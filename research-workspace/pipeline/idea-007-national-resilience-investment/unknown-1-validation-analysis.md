# Twelve signals that would confirm the resilience spending thesis

**The structural case for compounding multi-country national resilience spending through 2030+ rests on whether the current multipolar threat convergence triggers institutional lock-in mechanisms comparable to the early Cold War.** Against prior falsification evidence showing flat defense/GDP ratios and non-binding agreements, the validation research identifies twelve specific, observable confirmation signals — several of which have already partially triggered. The historical record shows that genuine spending regime changes share a common DNA: rapid institutional creation, bipartisan legislative embedding, geographic distribution of benefits, treaty obligations, and ongoing diffuse threat perception. The current moment exhibits most of these features, though the critical question is whether committed capital converts into sustained spending or stalls at the announcement stage.

The strongest confirmation evidence comes from three domains: Germany's constitutional debt brake reform (the highest possible form of institutional lock-in), the unprecedented quantum of sunk private-sector capital in US semiconductor fabs (**$500B+ committed**), and the DOD's direct equity stakes in critical minerals producers. The weakest evidence remains in the Japan-US-Australia trilateral pipeline, where MOUs still outnumber binding contracts — though China's April 2025 rare earth export bans appear to have triggered a genuine forcing function.

## Historical regime changes reveal the playbook for durable spending

Three historical episodes — the early Cold War, post-Sputnik, and post-9/11 — provide a diagnostic framework for evaluating whether current resilience spending will prove structural. The critical finding is that **multi-threat environments produce far more durable spending plateaus than single-threat episodes**, and the current moment shares key structural features with the early Cold War that post-9/11 lacked.

The early Cold War's "ratchet effect" is the definitive case. Pre-Korea defense spending sat at ~5% of GDP. The convergence of Soviet nuclear threat, Korean War, and European vulnerability drove spending to 14.2% of GDP — and it **never fell below ~8%** through the 1950s, even under Eisenhower, who explicitly tried to cut it and was the most credible person alive to do so. The mechanism was multi-threat simultaneity: demobilizing on one front was impossible when others remained active, and the institutional architecture created during the surge (CIA, NSC, NATO, unified DOD) generated self-reinforcing constituencies.

Post-9/11 spending followed a different pattern. DHS grew from **~$17B to $89B** over two decades and never declined in nominal terms, but the spending was operationally driven by a single threat category. When terrorism was perceived as manageable, defense/GDP ratios drifted down from 4.7% (2010) to ~3% (2018). The post-Sputnik case reveals an important caveat: NASA spending surged 15.9x in six years but declined sharply after the Moon landing, demonstrating that spending tied to a specific achievable goal with a "victory moment" proves less durable than spending against ongoing diffuse threats.

**Confirmation Signal #1 — Historical pattern match**: The current threat environment (China + Russia + Iran + supply chains simultaneously) structurally resembles the early Cold War more than post-9/11. If spending remains elevated across all theaters through 2028 without sequential threat resolution allowing demobilization on any front, this confirms the multi-threat compounding thesis. **Monitor**: Track whether a Russia-Ukraine settlement triggers European spending deceleration, which would be the most likely "single-threat collapse" scenario.

The speed of institutional creation is the earliest predictive indicator. In every historical episode, permanent agencies created within 18 months of a shock event proved nearly impossible to eliminate: the CIA and NSC (1947), NASA and DARPA (1958), and DHS (2003) all endure decades later. The current analogue — Space Force (2019), CHIPS Program Office, EU Commissioner for Defence and Space, European Critical Raw Materials Board — is already well advanced.

## Germany's constitutional amendment is the strongest lock-in signal globally

On **March 18, 2025**, the Bundestag voted **512-206** to amend the German Basic Law, permanently exempting defense spending above 1% of GDP from the constitutional debt brake. The Bundesrat approved it days later and President Steinmeier signed it into law. This is the highest possible form of institutional lock-in in a democratic system — reversing it requires a two-thirds supermajority, which is nearly impossible given that CDU, SPD, and Greens all supported it.

The amendment created a **€500 billion off-budget infrastructure fund** and enabled federal states to borrow up to 0.35% of GDP collectively. Germany's 2025 defense budget reached **€86.5 billion** (20.5% nominal increase YoY), with the 2026 budget approved at approximately **€108 billion**. The Finance Minister pledged **3.5% of GDP by 2029** — roughly doubling the defense budget to ~€162 billion.

The procurement pipeline confirms this is not announcement theater. Germany approved **€188.4 billion in major procurement projects in three years** (2023-2025), compared to €109 billion in the prior eight years (2015-2022). Specific committed contracts include 35 F-35A jets (with 15 more worth $2.9B under consideration), the €4 billion Arrow 3 missile defense system (first battery received December 2025), and Rheinmetall's involvement across 53 projects valued at €88 billion. A total planning pipeline of ~320 new projects carries an aggregate value of **~€438 billion**.

**Confirmation Signal #2 — German defense trajectory**: If Germany sustains defense spending above €100B annually through 2027 and the constitutional debt brake exemption is not challenged or reversed, this confirms irreversible institutional lock-in because the mechanism is constitutional, the procurement contracts are multi-year legal obligations, and the industrial base (Rheinmetall, Diehl, KMW) is now scaling capacity that creates employment dependencies. **Monitor**: Germany's annual defense budget in each Haushalt cycle; Rheinmetall quarterly order intake; Bundeswehr procurement acceleration act implementation.

## NATO's unanimous move to 5% of GDP creates alliance-level lock-in

For the first time since the target was established in 2014, **all 32 NATO allies** are expected to meet or exceed 2% of GDP on defense in 2025 — up from just three allies meeting the target in 2014. European NATO plus Canada spending reached an estimated **$607 billion in 2025**, having doubled in six years from approximately $300 billion. At the June 2025 Hague Summit, allies committed to **5% of GDP by 2035** (3.5% core defense plus 1.5% resilience/infrastructure).

The frontline states are spending at rates not seen since the Cold War. **Poland reached 4.48% of GDP** in 2025 and targets 5% by 2026 — the highest in NATO. Lithuania committed **4.0%** with plans for 5-6% through 2030. The Nordic countries collectively **more than doubled defense spending since 2020**, with Norway surpassing the United States in per-capita defense spending for the first time in NATO history. Finland's budget rose from €3.5 billion (2021) to €7.97 billion (2025), targeting 3% by 2029. Sweden's NATO accession in March 2024 is itself an irreversible institutional commitment.

The EU simultaneously launched **SAFE (Security Action for Europe)** — €150 billion in long-maturity bonds (up to 45 years) for joint defense procurement. Nineteen member states applied, and first disbursements of **€38 billion** were approved in February 2026. This represents the first-ever mutualization of European defense debt — an institutional innovation that creates infrastructure for ongoing joint borrowing.

**Confirmation Signal #3 — NATO spending momentum**: If European NATO spending exceeds 2.5% of GDP aggregate by 2028 and the SAFE loan program makes second-round disbursements, this confirms alliance-level compounding because the combination of peer pressure, treaty commitments, and joint debt issuance creates a ratchet that has historically proven resistant to reversal (NATO has endured 75+ years). **Monitor**: NATO annual defense expenditure reports; SAFE disbursement schedule; number of allies exceeding 3% of GDP.

## $500 billion in sunk semiconductor capital creates irreversible constituency

The quantum of committed private-sector capital for US semiconductor manufacturing now exceeds **$500 billion**, spread across six or more states including key swing states. This figure — confirmed by the Semiconductor Industry Association — represents the single largest industrial policy lock-in mechanism since the Cold War military-industrial complex.

TSMC's Arizona investment escalated from $12 billion (2020) to **$165 billion** (March 2025), the largest foreign greenfield investment in American history. Fab 1 is operational, producing chips for Apple (**100M+ chips** committed for 2026) and NVIDIA. Fab 2 is complete with equipment installation targeting Q3 2026. Micron committed **~$200 billion** across Idaho, New York, and Virginia. Samsung committed **$44 billion** in Taylor, Texas (91.8% construction complete). Intel committed **$28 billion** for Ohio (Phase 1 of a potential $100 billion megasite), with the US government taking an unprecedented **$8.9 billion equity stake** in the company.

The CHIPS Act itself has crossed a point of difficult reversal: **$30.9 billion in direct funding** awarded to 19 companies across 40 projects, with $6 billion already disbursed. The Trump administration's "One Big Beautiful Bill Act" (July 2025) **increased** the manufacturing tax credit from 25% to 35%, extending and enhancing incentives rather than repealing them. This bipartisan escalation under an administration initially hostile to the program is perhaps the strongest single data point for policy persistence.

**Confirmation Signal #4 — Semiconductor sunk cost lock-in**: If TSMC Fab 2 begins production on schedule in 2027 and Micron Idaho achieves first DRAM output, the sunk capital creates a **self-reinforcing political constituency** across 21 states and 125,000+ jobs that makes policy reversal economically irrational. The F-35 program model — deliberately spread across 45+ states to make cancellation impossible — is being replicated. **Monitor**: TSMC Arizona production ramp; Micron Idaho milestones; Samsung Taylor customer commitments; quarterly CHIPS disbursement reports from Commerce.

## DOD equity stakes in critical minerals producers are unprecedented

The Pentagon's July 2025 deal with MP Materials represents the most significant critical minerals commitment in US history. DOD purchased a **$400 million convertible preferred equity stake** (becoming MP's largest shareholder at 15%), provided a **$150 million loan** for heavy rare earth separation, guaranteed a **10-year NdPr price floor at $110/kg** (nearly double China's rate), and committed to a **10-year offtake for 7,000 MT/year of magnets**. JPMorgan Chase and Goldman Sachs provided **$1 billion in commercial financing** for MP's "10X Facility" — a $1.25 billion campus in Northlake, Texas targeting 10,000 MT/year of magnet capacity by 2028.

This creates the first fully integrated US rare earth mine-to-magnet supply chain in decades. MP's Independence Facility in Fort Worth commenced NdPr metal production in January 2025, with magnets expected by end of 2025 and GM committed to 1,000 MT. Heavy rare earth separation (dysprosium, terbium) at Mountain Pass targets commissioning mid-2026.

When the government holds direct equity, cancellation means destroying its own assets — an entirely different political calculus than merely ending a grant program. The DOE's Loan Programs Office was simultaneously rebranded as the "Office of Energy Dominance Financing" with **up to $250 billion in total loan guarantee authority** explicitly covering critical minerals extraction, refining, and processing.

**Confirmation Signal #5 — Government-as-shareholder lock-in**: If DOD's MP Materials equity stake generates returns and the 10X Facility achieves commissioning in 2028, this creates a template for government co-investment in strategic industries that is functionally irreversible. **Monitor**: MP Materials quarterly production reports; Mountain Pass heavy RE separation commissioning; DOD equity portfolio valuation; additional government equity deals in critical minerals.

## Japan's defense breakout hit 2% of GDP two years early

Japan's defense budget trajectory represents one of the clearest structural breaks in the dataset. After decades at the informal 1% of GDP ceiling, spending surged from **¥5.4 trillion (FY2022) to ¥9.04 trillion (FY2026)** — a 67% increase in four years. The 2% of GDP target was achieved two years ahead of the original FY2027 deadline, with Defense Minister Koizumi confirming the milestone in FY2025.

The five-year buildup program (FY2023-2027) allocated **¥43 trillion ($275 billion)** and is tracking on schedule. Spending categories include ¥970 billion for standoff missile capability, ¥160 billion for the GCAP next-generation fighter (with UK and Italy), and new allocations for defense industry base support and arms exports — capabilities Japan had explicitly eschewed for decades.

On critical minerals, Japan's Economic Security Promotion Act (ESPA) is fully operational with all four pillars in effect. PM Takaichi ordered amendment discussions in November 2025 to strengthen the Act further. JOGMEC — previously showing declining disbursements — has signed new concrete deals including a structured offtake MOU with US producer REAlloys (October 2025) and a joint investment of up to **€110 million** with Iwatani in French rare earth refining (Caremag/Carester). JOGMEC's cumulative Lynas investment reached **$384 million** ($250M in 2011 plus $134M in 2023), with Lynas now supplying 90% of Japan's light rare earths.

**Confirmation Signal #6 — Japan's structural break**: If Japan's FY2027 security strategy revision increases spending targets beyond the current five-year plan and JOGMEC disbursements show clear YoY increase from the ~¥86B baseline, this confirms Japan has permanently broken the 1% GDP ceiling. The key test is whether the Takaichi government's ESPA amendment expands critical minerals budgets with a ¥500B+ METI allocation. **Monitor**: Japan annual defense budget; JOGMEC quarterly deal announcements; ESPA amendment legislative progress; METI critical minerals budget line items.

## The Japan-US-Australia pipeline has five trackable milestones

The trilateral critical minerals pipeline between Japan, the US, and Australia is the thesis component where evidence was weakest in prior falsification research. The October 2025 frameworks — a US-Japan Critical Minerals Agreement and a US-Australia Critical Minerals Framework with an **$8.5 billion total commitment** — represent a qualitative upgrade from prior non-binding MOUs. The US-Australia framework includes a binding **six-month disbursement requirement** of at least $1 billion per country, with an initial $3 billion package announced immediately.

Physical processing capacity is under construction. Iluka Resources' **Eneabba Rare Earths Refinery** in Western Australia (backed by AU$1.65 billion in government loans) has commenced construction and targets 2026 commissioning — the first integrated non-Chinese rare earth refinery in the southern hemisphere. Arafura's **Nolans project** achieved final investment decision with AU$840 million in government funding and binding offtakes signed with Hyundai, Kia, and Siemens Gamesa. The **Alcoa Wagerup gallium refinery** — explicitly trilateral with US DOD backing plus Japanese participation through Sojitz — would produce 100 tonnes per year of gallium versus US annual consumption of just 21 tonnes.

South Korea's Korea Zinc **$7.4 billion Tennessee smelter** (with DOD holding a 40% stake via Crucible JV) will produce 13 critical minerals including gallium, germanium, and antimony. This is the single largest critical minerals processing investment globally, with operations beginning in 2029.

**Confirmation Signal #7 — Trilateral pipeline milestones**: Five specific events would confirm the pipeline is becoming real:

- US-Australia Mining/Minerals Investment Ministerial convened with verified $1B+ disbursements by April 2026
- Iluka Eneabba achieves first separated oxide production (NdPr + DyTb) by end of 2026
- Arafura Nolans breaks ground with confirmed financing close in H1 2026
- Alcoa Wagerup gallium FEED/construction contract awarded in 2026
- Korea Zinc Tennessee achieves first production phase by 2029

**Monitor**: Export Finance Australia quarterly reports; ASX announcements from Iluka (ILU) and Arafura (ARU); US EXIM disbursement confirmations; Korea Zinc construction updates.

## Bipartisan strange-bedfellow coalitions make spending cuts politically toxic

The multi-threat environment creates overlapping political coalitions that make resilience spending cuts electorally costly for any faction. Anti-China hawks drive Indo-Pacific military spending. Pro-Ukraine voices drive European defense support. Economic nationalists drive supply chain reshoring. Clean energy advocates support critical minerals for batteries and renewables. Labor unions support defense industrial base jobs. This coalition breadth — documented across bipartisan co-sponsorships (Slotkin D-MI with Ernst R-IA on the PRIMED Act; Warren D-NH with Scott R-SC on DPA reauthorization) — means that cutting spending alienates at least one part of any governing coalition.

The cross-administration continuity is striking. Trump's first term started critical minerals executive orders and created Space Force. Biden continued with IRA, BIL, and the first National Defense Industrial Strategy. Trump's second term dramatically accelerated with direct government equity stakes, expanded DPA authority, and enhanced tax credits. A Foreign Affairs analysis concluded there was "far more continuity between administrations than meets the eye" on China competition and supply chain resilience.

**Confirmation Signal #8 — Bipartisan persistence test**: If critical minerals and defense industrial base spending survives the next change in administration (whether 2028 or 2032) without structural cuts — following the historical pattern where Eisenhower couldn't reverse Truman's buildup and Obama maintained Bush's DHS — this is the definitive confirmation. **Monitor**: NDAA annual passage and defense topline; DPA Title III active project count (currently 56, up from 4 in 1994); bipartisan co-sponsorship counts on resilience legislation.

## China's export bans function as an accelerant, not a deterrent

China's April 2025 rare earth export controls created a forcing function that the thesis depends upon. Japan's rare earth dependency on China had actually *increased* from 58% (2020) to 63% (2024), showing that without acute pressure, diversification momentum reverses. The export bans changed the calculus overnight — the US-Australia $8.5B framework, DOD's MP Materials equity deal, and JOGMEC's new partnerships all followed within months.

The EU Critical Raw Materials Act (entered force May 2024) established binding 2030 targets: **10% domestic extraction, 40% domestic processing, 25% domestic recycling**, with a diversification cap of 65% from any single country. Sixty strategic projects have been selected across 13 EU members and 13 partner countries, with **€3 billion in near-term funding** via the December 2025 RESourceEU Action Plan.

**Confirmation Signal #10 — Crisis acceleration mechanism**: If future Chinese supply restrictions (additional minerals, tighter enforcement, crisis-triggered embargoes) trigger spending acceleration rather than paralysis, this confirms the thesis's core mechanism — that adversary actions strengthen rather than weaken the resilience spending coalition. The historical parallel is Korea validating NSC-68: the document was "politically dead" until an external shock made its recommendations inevitable. **Monitor**: Chinese rare earth export licensing data; allied stockpile purchase announcements following restrictions; emergency DPA Title III invocations.

## The EU Critical Raw Materials Act creates regulatory irreversibility

The CRMA (Regulation 2024/1252) enshrines binding processing and diversification targets in EU law with enforcement mechanisms, permitting timelines (24 months for extraction, 12 months for processing), and mandatory member state exploration programs due by May 2025. The European Critical Raw Materials Board and Centre create permanent institutional infrastructure. Projections show dependency on single-country rare earth extraction falling from **95% to 42%** and gallium dependency from **71% to 17%**.

However, the European Court of Auditors warned in February 2026 that diversification efforts were "unlikely to deliver targeted supply security on current trajectory" — flagging a real execution gap between regulatory ambition and operational reality.

**Confirmation Signal #11 — Regulatory execution test**: If the EU's first CRMA implementation report (due 2026) shows measurable progress toward 2030 targets and the second project selection round (January 2026) maintains or accelerates funding, this confirms regulatory lock-in is translating into industrial reality. **Monitor**: EU CRMA annual implementation reports; strategic project commissioning timelines; European Court of Auditors follow-up assessments.

## The fiscal constraint counterargument is the thesis's greatest vulnerability

The strongest argument against compounding through 2030+ is fiscal arithmetic. The US is attempting a defense buildup with debt/GDP above 100% and interest payments of **$881 billion** (2024) that now exceed the entire defense budget. During the early Cold War, debt/GDP was declining and the economy was growing rapidly — fiscal conditions were fundamentally more permissive. OMB's initial FY2026 defense request was essentially flat in real terms despite hawkish rhetoric, and Senate Armed Services Chair Wicker publicly criticized it as inadequate.

The honest assessment is that current evidence supports a spending plateau **modestly above recent baselines** — perhaps 3.5-4% of GDP in the US, 2.5-3% in Europe — rather than the dramatic step-change of the early Cold War (3% to 10%+). This still represents a meaningful structural shift for investors, but compounding through 2030+ requires both continued threat convergence and political willingness to accept fiscal trade-offs.

**Confirmation Signal #12 — Recession resilience test**: If defense and resilience spending survives the next economic downturn without proportional cuts — meaning the defense/GDP ratio rises during recession rather than falling — this would be the single strongest regime-change signal, because every post-Cold War recession saw defense spending targeted for cuts. **Monitor**: Defense spending trajectory during any GDP contraction; Congressional budget debates during fiscal stress; whether resilience spending is classified as discretionary or mandatory (mandatory spending is structurally stickier).

## Conclusion: a scorecard for tracking thesis materialization

The validation research identifies a structural case that is **confirming but not yet confirmed**. The institutional lock-in mechanisms are stronger than prior falsification research suggested — Germany's constitutional amendment, NATO's unanimous 5% commitment, $500B+ in sunk semiconductor capital, and DOD direct equity stakes in critical minerals producers represent qualitatively different commitments from the non-binding MOUs and flat spending lines that triggered falsification signals.

The key conviction-increase conditions can be tracked quarterly. Net new appropriations are verified: Japan's defense budget increased **67% in four years**, Germany's procurement pipeline tripled, and NATO Europe spending doubled in six years. Bilateral agreements are converting from MOUs to binding frameworks with disbursement timelines. Private sector CapEx explicitly citing government resilience incentives is at unprecedented scale. Multiple countries are showing coordinated increases, not just one.

What distinguishes the current moment from "this time is different" hopium is the **mechanism specificity**: constitutional amendments requiring supermajorities to reverse, government equity stakes that make cancellation self-destructive, multi-decade bond maturities that outlast electoral cycles, and cross-partisan coalitions that make spending cuts electorally costly for any faction. The historical playbook says these mechanisms — when they appear within 18 months of a shock — predict durable spending shifts. By that standard, the current cycle is well advanced. The critical unknown is whether threats persist simultaneously or resolve sequentially, because sequential resolution has historically enabled spending rollback that simultaneous convergence prevents.
