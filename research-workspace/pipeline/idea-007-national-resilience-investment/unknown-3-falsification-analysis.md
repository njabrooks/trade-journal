# Crisis spending commitments mostly don't survive — and this time may not be different

**The historical base rate is brutal for the thesis.** Every major defense spending surge in the past 75 years — post-Korea, post-Vietnam, post-Cold War, post-9/11 — was followed by significant reversion. Even the post-9/11 buildup, the strongest case for durable crisis spending, was slashed by sequestration within a decade. The post-Crimea NATO experience — where spending pledges went overwhelmingly unfulfilled for eight years until a second, larger shock forced compliance — represents the more common pattern. Current resilience commitments lack the institutional lock-in mechanisms (no new permanent agencies, no binding treaties, heavy reliance on annual appropriations and reversible executive orders) that would differentiate them from past episodes of fading urgency. Meanwhile, the CHIPS Act is under active presidential attack, IRA clean energy provisions have been legislatively gutted, Japan's defense tax was delayed three years, and China's critical minerals processing dominance has actually *increased* despite diversification rhetoric.

---

## Finding 1: Every US defense buildup since WWII has been followed by major reversion

**Source type:** Historical defense budget data, SIPRI, CSIS  
**Credibility:** High — based on official budget statistics  

US defense spending as a share of GDP shows an unbroken pattern of post-crisis decline. The Korean War peak of **~15% GDP** fell to ~10% under Eisenhower. Vietnam's **9.5%** (1968) dropped to **5.5%** by 1979. The Reagan buildup peaked at **6.8%** (1986) and fell to **3.0%** by 2001. The post-9/11 surge reached **5.7%** (2010) before sequestration drove it back to **3.1%** by 2016 — approaching pre-9/11 levels. The secular trend is a staircase down: each successive peak has been lower than the last. **100% of major buildups were walked back.**

The post-9/11 case deserves particular scrutiny because it is the thesis's strongest historical analogue. Despite the creation of DHS, the Authorization for Use of Military Force, and active combat operations in two theaters, the Budget Control Act of 2011 imposed **$1 trillion in defense cuts over ten years**. Sequestration cut defense spending by ~$85 billion in FY2013 alone. The active Army shrank from 566,000 (2011) to **476,000 (2016) — smaller than the pre-9/11 force**. DoD R&D contract spending fell **53%** between 2009 and 2015. The mechanism designed to be "so injurious to the military it would never go into effect" (Defense Secretary Mattis) went into effect anyway.

**Bearing on thesis:** Strongly contradicts. The thesis's claim that spending will "structurally compound through 2030+" faces a 0-for-6 historical record on sustained buildups.

## Finding 2: Post-Crimea NATO pledges failed for eight years — the dominant pattern

**Source type:** NATO official data, Carnegie Endowment, Heritage Foundation  
**Credibility:** High — official NATO statistics  

The 2014 Wales Summit pledge to spend 2% of GDP on defense by 2024 was the most significant collective defense spending commitment since the Cold War. The results were catastrophic for the persistence thesis. Only **3 of 27 allies** met the target in 2014. By 2022 — eight years later and on the eve of Russia's full-scale invasion — only **7 of 30 allies** (23%) had reached 2%. Heritage Foundation calculates European NATO members collectively **underfunded defense by $827.9 billion** relative to the 2% target over the period. Belgium openly renounced the pledge shortly after signing. Carnegie Endowment concluded in 2015 that it was "highly unrealistic that all 28 NATO allies will ever reach the 2 percent spending goal."

Only countries directly threatened by Russia (the Baltics, Poland) sustained meaningful increases. Western European allies — Germany, France, Italy, Spain — largely ignored the pledge for eight years. **It required a second, far more dramatic crisis (the 2022 full-scale invasion) to produce the compliance that the first crisis failed to generate.** The compound annual growth rate of real NATO defense spending from 2014 to 2022 was a meager **1.3%**. The post-Crimea case demonstrates that crisis-driven pledges without active military operations or imminent existential threat erode rapidly.

**Bearing on thesis:** Strongly contradicts. This is the single most relevant modern analogue and shows clear spending-pledge failure.

## Finding 3: Current commitments lack the institutional lock-in of post-9/11

**Source type:** Legal analysis, congressional records, NATO treaty text  
**Credibility:** High — based on statutory and treaty language  

The post-9/11 spending durability was underpinned by three structural mechanisms: a **new permanent cabinet-level agency** (DHS, with 240,000 employees and its own budget line), a **standing legal authorization for military force** (AUMF), and **active combat operations** creating irreducible spending demand. Current resilience commitments lack all three.

**No new permanent agency exists.** The CHIPS Program Office operates within NIST/Commerce — and approximately **120 of its ~150 employees were fired** through DOGE actions. A companion R&D institution (SMART USA) had its $285 million contract terminated. Critical minerals policy runs through executive orders and existing agencies with no standalone appropriation. Trump's National Energy Dominance Council is a coordinating body, not a permanent bureaucracy.

**No binding treaty obligations exist.** The NATO 2% pledge is explicitly **not legally binding** — the Atlantic Council notes that "politicians in democratic countries making budgetary promises for successors a decade down the road without any sort of binding mechanism is a poor recipe for success." AUKUS is **not a formal treaty** ratified by the Senate; it is structured through executive agreements and implementing legislation subject to annual re-funding. Trump ordered a review of AUKUS in mid-2025.

**All US defense spending is 100% discretionary**, requiring annual congressional re-appropriation. The CHIPS Act's $174 billion "Science" portion was **only authorized, not appropriated** — actual appropriations fell **42% short** for NSF alone in FY2024. The Federation of American Scientists found an aggregate **$8 billion+ shortfall** across CHIPS research agencies. Critical minerals policy is almost entirely executive-order-based and fully reversible.

**Bearing on thesis:** Strongly contradicts the conviction-increase condition that "institutional lock-in" mechanisms exist comparable to post-9/11.

## Finding 4: Japan's economic security implementation lags rhetoric by years

**Source type:** Academic research (Brookings, Columbia/CJEB), IMF, CSIS, legal analysis  
**Credibility:** High — peer-reviewed and institutional research  

Japan's Economic Security Promotion Act (2022) was enacted May 2022, but key pillars did not become operational until **May 2024** — two full years later. The companion security clearance system passed separately in 2024 and won't be fully operational until 2025. Researchers at Brookings, ANU, and Waseda concluded that "when it comes to the objective of strengthening the resilience of supply chains, **there is little evidence that Japan has made much of a dent** in abating the risks of supplier concentration." Japan's dependence on Chinese rare earth imports actually **increased from 50% in 2014 to 70% in 2022**, directly contradicting the ESPA's stated goals.

Japan's **2% of GDP defense target is misleading**: Brookings researcher Adam Liff demonstrated that the actual MOD defense budget target for FY2027 is approximately **1.5% of GDP**, with the remainder classified as Coast Guard, public infrastructure, and science spending. "No pledge to double Japan's defense budget appears anywhere in the three documents," Liff wrote. The defense tax hikes to fund the buildup were postponed repeatedly — originally proposed for 2024, then delayed to 2026, and finally agreed upon in December 2025 for implementation starting in **2027**, five years after the original commitment.

Japan's historical track record is sobering. The informal **1% of GDP defense spending cap held for 46 years** (1976-2022) despite being formally abolished by PM Nakasone in 1987, declared non-binding by PM Abe in 2017, and reaffirmed as lifted by Defense Minister Kishi in 2021. Even under Abe — Japan's most hawkish modern prime minister — actual defense spending never exceeded 1%. The Rapidus semiconductor project faces a **¥3.3 trillion ($23 billion) funding gap** with private equity investment from founding companies totaling a mere **¥7.3 billion ($51 million)** — a 235:1 government-to-private investment ratio.

**Bearing on thesis:** Partially contradicts. Institutional infrastructure exists (ESPA, new METI division) but implementation is slow, targets are inflated, and historical follow-through is poor.

## Finding 5: Post-COVID supply chain commitments show massive announcement-to-delivery gaps

**Source type:** Government disbursement data, European Court of Auditors, industry tracking  
**Credibility:** High — official auditing bodies and government data  

**The CHIPS Act has disbursed roughly $1.1 billion in FY2025 outlays against $39 billion in manufacturing incentives** — a greater than 95% gap between commitment and actual spending. Intel, the flagship recipient at $7.86 billion, delayed its Ohio fab from a 2025 operating date to **2030-2031** — a five-to-six-year delay. Ohio officials privately worried Intel could become "another Foxconn scandal." Samsung's $44 billion Taylor, Texas fab is 91.8% structurally complete but has **no chipmaking equipment installed and no customers**, with the original 4nm process now obsolete. TSMC's Arizona fabs are running one to two years behind schedule with construction costs **4-5x higher than Taiwan**.

The **European Court of Auditors** concluded the EU Chips Act is "very unlikely to be sufficient to reach the overly ambitious Digital Decade target" of doubling Europe's semiconductor market share to 20%. The EU had set an identical 20% target in 2013 — by 2019, Europe's share was **3%, unchanged from 2015**. Intel's Magdeburg, Germany fab (€30 billion, €9.9 billion in subsidies) has been delayed from 2023 to **2029-2030**, with the German Institute for Economic Research assessing a **maximum 50% chance** it proceeds at all.

Critical minerals diversification has gone backward: IEA data shows China's processing concentration **rose from 82% in 2020 to 86% in 2024**. Investment momentum weakened in 2024, with spending rising just **5% (2% inflation-adjusted)**, down from 14% in 2023. Exploration activity plateaued. The IRA's clean energy provisions have been the most dramatic reversal: **$34.8 billion in projects were cancelled, closed, or downsized in 2025** — nearly three times the $12.3 billion in new investment announced during the same period. In two quarters of 2025, cancellations exceeded new announcements.

**Bearing on thesis:** Strongly contradicts. The announcement-to-delivery gap is consistently large across all categories of resilience spending.

## Finding 6: European defense spending has genuinely increased but faces a 2028 fiscal cliff

**Source type:** NATO data, EU Council records, Bruegel economic analysis  
**Credibility:** High — official EU and NATO sources  

NATO reports that all **32 members are projected to meet 2% of GDP in 2025** — a genuine milestone after 11 years of failure. This is the strongest evidence for the thesis. However, critical caveats undermine the durability story. First, **16 allies (half the alliance) are barely at 2.0-2.1%** of GDP — just scraping over the line. Second, some countries reached 2% partly through **GDP denominator effects** (slow growth shrinks the denominator) and **reclassification of security expenditures** rather than genuine capability increases. Germany's Zeitenwende €100 billion special fund is being spent but is **one-off and temporary**, expected to be fully depleted by 2027. The regular defense budget was frozen at ~€50 billion through 2026, creating an annual gap of **€25-30 billion** to sustain 2% without the special fund.

The EU's approach creates a specific fiscal cliff. **Seventeen member states activated the Stability and Growth Pact's national escape clause** to accommodate defense spending — but this is temporary, expiring by **2028**. Bruegel analysis warns that after expiry, "higher defense spending will require additional fiscal adjustment of **0.4 percentage points of GDP**" — meaning either cuts elsewhere or tax increases. **Eight EU member states are already in Excessive Deficit Procedures**, including France. Poland's finance minister openly criticized the fiscal rules as incompatible with defense spending needs.

The new NATO target of **5% of GDP by 2035** (3.5% core defense + 1.5% resilience) faces even steeper skepticism. Only **three countries** (Poland at 4.48%, Lithuania at 4.0%, Latvia at 3.73%) currently meet even the 3.5% core target. Spain explicitly opted out. SIPRI concluded that "the fiscal sustainability of several [NATO members] is questionable."

**Bearing on thesis:** Mixed. Genuine progress on 2%, but structural fiscal constraints and the 2028 escape-clause expiry threaten durability. The 5% target appears aspirational.

## Finding 7: Fiscal arithmetic across all key countries works against sustained spending

**Source type:** CBO, IMF, OECD fiscal projections  
**Credibility:** High — official fiscal forecasting bodies  

The fiscal constraints across the thesis's key countries are severe and worsening. US debt held by the public is projected to rise from **100% of GDP (2025) to 118% (2035)**, surpassing the WWII record in 2029. With the One Big Beautiful Bill Act factored in, NBER projects debt reaching **183% of GDP by 2054**. Net interest costs already match their post-WWII record at **3.2% of GDP ($970 billion)** and are projected to rise to **4.1% of GDP ($1.8 trillion) by 2035**. Interest payments are already the **second-largest line item in the federal budget**, exceeding both defense and Medicare. CBO projects that discretionary spending — including defense — will **decline as a share of GDP** as mandatory spending and interest costs grow.

Japan carries **~240% debt-to-GDP** with social security consuming one-third of the budget and rising. The AMRO estimates Japan could breach its debt threshold by **2033-2034**. IMF called for "growth-friendly fiscal adjustment starting in 2026" and warned debt servicing costs are rising as Japan exits ultra-low interest rates. Carnegie Endowment concluded that "Japan's fiscal condition is no longer merely an economic concern — **it has become a national security issue.**" CSIS found "the fiscal and political constraints around continued defense spending growth are very real."

European countries face the EU's fiscal rulebook: the SGP limits deficits to 3% GDP and debt to 60% GDP. The escape clause for defense is temporary. Roll Call notes the gulf between then and now: "In 1970, when Bonn spent 3% on defense, the German economy was growing at 5.1%. This year the German economy is not expected to grow at all."

**Bearing on thesis:** Strongly contradicts. Sovereign debt trajectories create a structural ceiling on discretionary spending growth across all key countries.

## Finding 8: Active political rollback of resilience spending is already underway

**Source type:** Legislative records, executive actions, news reporting  
**Credibility:** High — documented government actions  

The thesis assumes spending survives electoral cycles. Current evidence suggests the opposite. **Trump called the CHIPS Act "a horrible, horrible thing"** in his March 2025 address to Congress, urging Congress to "get rid of" it. The CHIPS Program Office was gutted — approximately 80% of staff fired. The Commerce Department voided **$7.4 billion in CHIPS R&D funding** for the Natcast semiconductor research center. The CHIPS office was transferred to a new "US Investment Accelerator" with a mandate to renegotiate awards downward. Foley & Lardner assessed that "a second CHIPS Act is unlikely to gain traction" given no appetite among congressional Republicans for another large spending bill.

The Inflation Reduction Act's clean energy provisions experienced the most dramatic reversal. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 4, 2025) **repealed or accelerated the sunset of major IRA tax credits**: EV credits repealed after September 2025, clean hydrogen terminated seven years early, residential clean energy credits terminated after December 2025, wind and solar credits curtailed. Over **$5 billion in unobligated IRA funds were rescinded** from DOE programs. BloombergNEF forecasts clean energy installations will **plunge 41% after 2027**. The Pentagon itself proposed cutting **8% of its budget annually** for five years (~$50 billion/year) to "redirect" toward new priorities.

Even bipartisan support is thinner than it appears. The original CHIPS Act vote saw **majorities of Republicans in both chambers oppose the bill** (Senate 64-33, House 243-187). In Australia, only **33% of the public supports increasing the defense budget**, and AUKUS support within the Labor caucus is described as "a mile wide and an inch deep." Former prime ministers from both major parties (Keating, Turnbull) have attacked AUKUS. Australia rejected the US demand for 3.5% GDP defense spending in June 2025.

**Bearing on thesis:** Strongly contradicts. Resilience spending is being actively cut, not compounding, across multiple categories in the world's largest economy.

## Finding 9: European populist parties threaten defense spending consensus

**Source type:** Polling data, party platforms, Atlantic Council analysis  
**Credibility:** Medium-high — polling data and party statements  

For the first time in modern history, far-right populist parties are simultaneously leading polls in Europe's three largest economies. **AfD** in Germany (polling first as of August 2025) has a plurality of members (42%) worried about spending too much on defense at the expense of other public spending. **National Rally** in France shows a similar pattern (43% expressing concern). Both oppose EU-level military buildups. **Reform UK** has gained significant ground in Britain. The Atlantic Council warns that "populist gains are threatening Europe's strategic coherence."

Spain explicitly rejected the NATO 5% target, requesting a formal exemption and capping defense at 2.1%. The conceptual structure of the 5% target — split into 3.5% "core" and 1.5% "security-related" — creates what the Atlantic Council calls "conceptual ambiguity with practical consequences," ripe for creative accounting rather than genuine capability investment. Southern European states reportedly reached 2% largely through reclassifying existing security expenditures. Trump's own questioning of Article 5 ("depends on your definition") paradoxically undermines the rationale for the very European defense spending the thesis depends upon.

**Bearing on thesis:** Moderately contradicts. Electoral cycles could bring to power parties that explicitly oppose defense spending increases across key European countries.

## Finding 10: The defense spending ratchet effect is weaker than for social spending

**Source type:** Academic literature (Peacock-Wiseman, Higgs)  
**Credibility:** Medium-high — established academic frameworks  

The Peacock-Wiseman (1961) ratchet effect — where crisis-driven public spending persists at elevated levels after the crisis passes — is often invoked to support the thesis. But the evidence shows this effect operates **much more weakly for defense than for social spending**. European social spending more than doubled from 1991 to 2023 and never reverted. Defense spending, by contrast, has been walked back after every crisis. The key asymmetry: social programs create **mass beneficiary constituencies** (pensioners, healthcare recipients) who resist cuts, while defense spending creates narrower industrial interests that are politically weaker. Robert Higgs's "Crisis and Leviathan" (1987) found the ratchet effect strongest for expansions of regulatory and administrative state power, not necessarily for defense spending levels.

Defense's share of the US federal budget fell from **72% (1952) to ~15-16% today** as entitlements grew — a relentless, structural squeeze that no crisis has permanently reversed. The Pentagon proposed its own 8% annual cuts, demonstrating that even the defense establishment accepts the fiscal reality of competing priorities.

**Bearing on thesis:** Moderately contradicts. The structural dynamics favor entitlement growth over defense/resilience spending persistence.

**Overall Kill Condition: PARTIALLY TRIGGERED.** The historical base rate strongly supports the skeptical case — crisis-driven spending commitments are more often walked back than sustained. Current institutional lock-in is markedly weaker than the post-9/11 architecture. However, certain features of the current moment prevent a full trigger: the active war in Ukraine creates ongoing threat perception unlike post-Crimea; Germany's constitutional reform is genuinely structural; bipartisan support exists in Japan (if not on funding mechanisms); and private sector investment in semiconductors, while behind schedule, represents real sunk costs. The kill condition is closest to being triggered on the historical precedent element and the absence of DHS-equivalent agencies, and furthest from being triggered on the question of whether the current geopolitical environment sustains urgency.

## The current moment most closely resembles a hybrid — and that's the problem

The thesis implicitly argues the current moment resembles the post-9/11 pattern of durable spending. The adversarial evidence suggests it more closely resembles a **hybrid between post-9/11 and post-Crimea** — with the critical question being whether the geopolitical threat environment remains acute enough to prevent the post-Crimea pattern of fading urgency.

Three features distinguish the current moment from post-Crimea and favor persistence: active conflict in Ukraine maintaining threat salience; China's assertiveness creating a second, reinforcing threat axis; and some genuine institutional infrastructure (Japan's ESPA, Germany's constitutional reform, CHIPS Act appropriations). Three features resemble post-Crimea and favor reversion: **no direct military engagement** by most NATO allies or AUKUS partners; fiscal constraints far worse than 2014 (US debt at 100% vs. 74% GDP; Japan at 240%); and active political opposition (Trump attacking CHIPS Act, European populists opposing defense spending, Australian public skepticism on AUKUS).

The critical vulnerability is that current commitments depend on **sustained threat perception without direct military involvement** — precisely the condition under which post-Crimea commitments evaporated. If Ukraine reaches a frozen conflict, if US-China tensions de-escalate, or if a global recession forces fiscal austerity, the historical pattern suggests spending will revert. The post-9/11 pattern was sustained by active combat operations creating irreducible spending demand; no equivalent exists today for most countries in the thesis.

## Caveats and limitations

This analysis carries several important limitations. First, the **adversarial framing** means evidence for persistence was deprioritized; a balanced assessment would give more weight to genuine structural changes like Germany's constitutional reform. Second, **historical analogies are imperfect** — the simultaneous convergence of European land war, US-China strategic competition, pandemic-exposed supply chain fragility, and energy security concerns is genuinely unprecedented in combination, even if individual elements have precedents. Third, **time horizon matters**: most of the evidence shows spending eventually reverts, but the thesis only needs persistence through 2030+, roughly four to five more years — a shorter window than typical reversion cycles. Fourth, **private sector sunk costs** (TSMC Arizona, Samsung Taylor, European defense orders) create constituencies for continued government support even if political will wanes. Fifth, much data on the most recent commitments (2024-2026) reflects early implementation — disbursement lags are normal for large government programs and don't necessarily indicate abandonment. Finally, **the Trump administration's approach is unusually hostile** to certain industrial-policy mechanisms (CHIPS Act, IRA) while simultaneously hawkish on defense — representing a reshuffling rather than wholesale abandonment of resilience spending.

## Synthesis: the weight of evidence favors the skeptic

The falsification research reveals a thesis under significant stress. The **historical base rate** is the most damaging finding: no major defense spending surge in 75 years has been sustained without reversion, and the single most relevant recent precedent (post-Crimea) saw near-total pledge failure for eight years. The **institutional lock-in** that distinguished post-9/11 durability — permanent new agencies, legal authorizations, active military operations — is largely absent from current commitments. The **announcement-to-delivery gap** is enormous across CHIPS Act, EU Chips Act, IRA, and critical minerals, with disbursements running years behind and several flagship projects in crisis. **Active political rollback** is already underway in the world's largest economy, with the sitting president calling the flagship industrial policy bill "horrible" and signing legislation gutting clean energy provisions. And **fiscal arithmetic** works relentlessly against sustained increases: with US interest payments already the second-largest budget item and projected to nearly double, EU escape clauses expiring in 2028, and Japan at 240% debt-to-GDP, the structural capacity for spending increases is constrained and deteriorating.

The strongest counterpoint to the adversarial case is that **the geopolitical threat environment is genuinely more acute and multi-dimensional** than post-Crimea. Ukraine, China, supply chains, and energy security create reinforcing pressures that may sustain political will longer than any single crisis. But the evidence from post-9/11 — where even active combat, a new cabinet-level department, and standing military authorization couldn't prevent a decade of subsequent cuts — suggests that fiscal gravity eventually overcomes even the strongest political commitments. The question is not whether crisis-driven spending will compound structurally through 2030+, but whether it will survive long enough to create irreversible industrial facts on the ground before the next fiscal consolidation cycle arrives.
