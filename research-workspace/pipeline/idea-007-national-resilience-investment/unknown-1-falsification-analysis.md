# National resilience spending fails the incrementality test

**The thesis that multi-country "national resilience" spending represents genuinely new, structurally compounding government capital is substantially contradicted by the evidence.** Across all three focal countries — Japan, the United States, and Australia — the gap between announced commitments and actual disbursements is wide and growing. Defense spending as a share of GDP is flat or declining in every case; bilateral agreements are non-binding frameworks rather than procurement contracts; and the key financial institutions cited as pipeline operators (JOGMEC, JBIC) show declining, not accelerating, disbursements. Four of five kill conditions are at least partially met. The strongest version of the thesis — that this spending will structurally compound through 2030+ and survive electoral cycles — faces formidable historical and fiscal counterevidence.

---

## Japan's 2% target relies on accounting reclassification, not new money

The single most damaging piece of evidence against the thesis comes from Japan's defense budget arithmetic. Brookings Institution researchers documented definitively that "nowhere in Japan's December 2022 three documents did the government announce plans to raise the 'defense budget' from 1% to 2% of GDP." The actual Ministry of Defense budget increase from FY2022 to the FY2027 target is **65%, not 100%**. The gap is filled by reclassifying Coast Guard spending, public infrastructure, private-sector science and technology investments, and SDF pensions under a new "Comprehensive Defense Architecture" umbrella.

SIPRI confirmed that "even using Japan's GDP in 2022, the current planned military budget of 8.9 trillion yen for 2027 would represent **only 1.5% of GDP**." The government needs over ¥2 trillion annually from non-defense budget lines to reach the headline figure. CSIS, generally supportive of the Japan buildup, acknowledged that "Japan is using some creative accounting to achieve the 2 percent target."

The denominator game compounds the problem. Japan calculates its percentage using **FY2022 GDP (~¥550 trillion)** rather than projected current GDP. Nippon.com reported that the FY2025 initial budget of ¥9.9 trillion represents "1.8% of the fiscal 2022 GDP and 1.6% of the projected GDP for fiscal 2025." Bloomberg headlined that GDP growth and yen dynamics were "stretching" the 2% goal further out of reach. The financing mechanism remains unresolved — income tax increases to fund defense were pushed to 2027, and public support erodes when talk turns to tax hikes.

On critical minerals specifically, METI's total budget is rising (~1.5x for FY2026), but this is the entire ministry budget, not economic security alone. PM Takaichi's November 2025 economic package identified 17 "designated strategic areas" without clear line-item allocations for each. A new "subsidy review mechanism" suggests fiscal space is being reclaimed from existing programs rather than generated incrementally.

## JBIC and JOGMEC disbursements are declining, not accelerating

The thesis describes a Japan-US-Australia trilateral critical minerals pipeline as "already operational." The financial data contradicts this directly.

**JBIC total commitments collapsed from ¥2.6 trillion in FY2020 to ¥1.5 trillion in FY2024** — a 42% decline over four years. Natural resource loans fell 18.5% year-over-year in FY2024 (¥481 billion to ¥392 billion). Of FY2024 natural resource lending, only ¥135 billion went to copper ore — the only identifiable critical minerals category. JBIC disbursements show no upward trend: ¥2.1 trillion in FY2020, ¥1.9 trillion in FY2024. H1 FY2025 data (¥756 billion in commitments) suggests another year at depressed levels.

JOGMEC's critical minerals budget is modest by any measure. Its cumulative investment since 2004 exceeds $600 million across 100+ projects — averaging roughly **$30 million per year**. Individual investments remain small: CAD$17-20 million in Namibia, €110 million jointly in France, $134 million in Lynas. No evidence emerged of a dramatic year-over-year budget increase for critical minerals specifically. In Australia, JOGMEC's activities consist primarily of MOUs and standard joint-venture structures — the Northern Territory agreements signed circa 2024 are explicitly memoranda of understanding, not binding investment commitments. ASPI's assessment is blunt: "Policy tools exist, but they're applied episodically."

## Bilateral frameworks are non-binding, and no trilateral agreement exists

The Takaichi-Trump summits (October 2025, March 2026) produced four documents, none of which constitute binding procurement contracts. Sullivan & Cromwell explicitly described the critical minerals agreement as a **"non-binding common policy framework."** RUSI assessed the visit as "less about treaty-level commitments and more about clarifying political signals and consolidating bilateral stability — a pragmatic showcase of diplomacy rather than grand strategy."

The headline $550 billion "investment pledge" consists entirely of private-sector commitments by Japanese corporations (Toyota, GE Vernova/Hitachi), not government appropriations. A Syracuse University analyst noted Japan "was forced to pledge investments in the U.S. for the whopping amount of $550 billion — an amount the Japanese government and corporations can hardly spare." The second tranche (~$73 billion announced at the October summit) covers commercial nuclear and gas generation projects.

**No formal trilateral Japan-US-Australia critical minerals agreement exists.** The US signed separate bilateral frameworks with Australia (October 20, 2025) and Japan (October 27, 2025). The only truly trilateral project identified is the Alcoa-Sojitz-JOGMEC gallium recovery facility in Western Australia — a single project producing 100 tons/year of gallium. The Quad Critical Minerals Initiative, launched July 2025, was described by the Lowy Institute as testing whether the Quad "isn't just a talking shop" and by IEEFA as "a signal of shared intent, but one still in search of substance." Even the enabling legislation (Critical Minerals Partnership Act, S.2550) remains an unenacted bill authorizing only $50 million for FY2026.

Further undermining urgency, the US and China agreed in October 2025 to a one-year pause on Chinese rare earth export controls — potentially reducing the political case for allied supply chain diversification.

## The US defense budget is flat in real terms, and resilience programs are being gutted

The FY2026 base discretionary defense budget request of **$892.6 billion is identical to the FY2025 enacted level** — a real-terms cut after inflation and the fifth consecutive year of flat-to-declining base budgets. Senator Roger Wicker, the Republican chair of the Armed Services Committee, called it "a cut in real terms." Defense spending as a share of GDP fell from **3.3% in FY2024 to an estimated 2.9% in FY2025**. CBO projects "little real growth in the department's base-budget costs through 2029."

The "$1 trillion defense budget" headline relies on $113 billion in mandatory reconciliation funding from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act — one-time money available across FY2025-FY2029, not recurring base growth. Without this supplement, DoD R&D spending would **decrease by $16.1 billion (18%)** compared to FY2025. RDT&E grew only 1% ($780 million) in the base request. DARPA's budget trajectory is essentially flat. Space Force RDT&E was cut 17%.

The broader Biden-era resilience apparatus is being systematically dismantled. The DOE Loan Programs Office lost approximately **75% of its funding** and 58.5% of its staff. Over $11 billion in unobligated IRA funding was rescinded through the reconciliation law. DOE cancelled $6.8 billion in Bipartisan Infrastructure Law awards and $2.5 billion in IRA awards. Major clean energy tax credits (30D clean vehicles, 45V hydrogen) were eliminated. The Advanced Technology Vehicle Manufacturing Loan Program authority was repealed entirely.

Critical minerals represent the one area of genuine new US spending — $2 billion for the National Defense Stockpile, a $400 million Pentagon equity stake in MP Materials, and active use of DPA Title III for 13 defense-critical minerals. But even here, $2 billion was diverted from CHIPS Act semiconductor research — robbing Peter to pay Paul. DOGE identified approximately **$11.1 billion in Pentagon "efficiencies"** (workforce reductions), and DISA's critical IT systems faced "extreme risk for loss of service" after DOGE-driven departures. The same administration promoting "resilience" simultaneously degraded core resilience functions.

## Australia's spending is stuck in a narrow band, and flagship projects are failing

Australia's defense spending has **hovered between 1.9% and 2.1% of GDP since 2017-18** with no breakout. The current level of 2.05% falls below the 2016 Defence White Paper projection of 2.21-2.39% by this year. Real growth in the 2025-26 budget was only 1.0% after inflation. ASPI called it "sadly, an opportunity lost." When the US requested 3.5% of GDP, Australia rejected the push.

AUKUS submarine spending illustrates the cannibalization problem. Of the $50.3 billion announced over the decade, $6 billion was previously earmarked for submarine programs and $3 billion came from "savings" within the existing Defence portfolio. Defense analyst Marcus Hellyer stated plainly: **"$10.6 billion in additional funding over the forward estimates is not new money."** Core defense funding, excluding foreign exchange compensation, actually declined in real terms between 2023-24 and 2025-26 — from A$154.0 billion to A$152.5 billion.

On critical minerals, the 2023 Critical Minerals Strategy was immediately criticized by Allens (a major law firm) as providing "no additional material government financial investment or tax incentives." The A$5 billion Critical Minerals Facility is highly concentrated — **33% went to a single project** (Iluka Eneabba) experiencing 42-50% cost overruns, with commissioning delayed from 2025-26 to 2027 and Iluka posting A$288 million in net losses. The A$1.2 billion Critical Minerals Strategic Reserve announced in April 2025 remains in a design phase, with the Foundation Phase allocating only **A$35 million** for taskforce establishment. The Critical Minerals Production Tax Incentive (A$7 billion over a decade) doesn't begin until FY2027/28. Multiple lithium and nickel mines paused production in 2024 due to low prices.

## Private sector CapEx is driven by AI hype and VC capital, not resilience incentives

The thesis requires identifiable private-sector capital expenditure acceleration citing government resilience incentives as catalysts. The evidence points to different drivers.

Palantir's explosive growth (70% YoY revenue growth in Q4 2025) is driven by **AI Platform commercial adoption** — US commercial revenue grew 121% YoY versus government revenue at 52%. Its 67.9x forward price-to-sales ratio reflects AI hype, not resilience fundamentals. Anduril's $60 billion valuation is funded by **$6.5 billion in venture capital**, and the company is operating at losses exceeding $1 billion annually. This is a VC-backed startup model, not government-incentive-catalyzed private investment.

Traditional defense primes show modest, formulaic CapEx patterns. L3Harris maintains guidance at approximately 2% of sales — flat and routine. RTX's CapEx is rising from $2.9 billion to $3.1 billion (roughly 7%), driven by existing contract backlogs ($193.6 billion), not new resilience programs. Stock buybacks have been curtailed under political pressure from Trump rather than redirected by incentive structures.

Critical minerals companies present the most damaging counterevidence. Despite **$288 million in DoD funding**, Lynas Rare Earths' Texas heavy rare earth processing facility halted construction due to permitting failures — the company is pivoting to expand Malaysian operations instead. MP Materials is operating at a loss (adjusted EBITDA of -$50.2 million in 2024) and has become increasingly government-dependent: the Pentagon took a $400 million equity stake, guaranteed floor prices, and committed to purchase 100% of its magnets. This is government subsidy substituting for market viability, not private CapEx acceleration. The GAO found that "there is little appetite in industry to take on financial risk" in critical minerals, and IEA data shows top-three processing regions' market share actually **increased from 82% to 86%** between 2020 and 2025.

## History and fiscal math make structural compounding implausible

Every major US defense buildup in the past 70 years has been followed by a drawdown: post-Korea (-28%), post-Vietnam (-40%), post-Cold War (-28%), post-Iraq/Afghanistan (sequestration, ~10% across-the-board cuts). The post-Cold War peace dividend alone captured an estimated **$2.8 trillion in foregone defense spending** and **€4.2 trillion in Europe** (Bruegel estimate). The pattern is unbroken.

The fiscal constraints are binding. CBO projects US federal debt reaching **108% of GDP by 2030** and 120% by 2036. Net interest costs ($1.0 trillion in FY2026) already exceed national defense spending ($885 billion). Japan carries the world's highest debt-to-GDP ratio at approximately 240%. S&P Global Ratings estimated that meeting even the 3.5% NATO "core" defense target could add roughly **$2 trillion to European collective government debt by 2035**. S&P analyst Frank Gill called it "completely illusionary to believe that governments in Europe could save on public pensions, on healthcare, on care more generally" — and aging populations make these costs structurally rising.

NATO's new 5% spending target (3.5% core + 1.5% "resilience") is explicitly designed for creative accounting. Italy attempted to classify a €13.8 billion Strait of Messina bridge as defense spending. The UK includes war pensions. Germany counts interest payments on past military debt and budgets from seven ministries. The Atlantic Council warned that "the 1.5 percent risks morphing into a bookkeeping loophole." No enforcement mechanism exists.

## Conclusion: what the falsification exercise reveals

The evidence strongly satisfies four of five kill conditions. Defense-plus-infrastructure spending as a share of GDP is flat or declining across all three countries (Kill Condition 1). The Takaichi-Trump frameworks are explicitly non-binding (Kill Condition 2). JBIC disbursements have declined 42% since FY2020 (Kill Condition 3). The US base defense budget is at CBO baseline or below, with real-terms cuts for five consecutive years (Kill Condition 4). Private-sector CapEx acceleration is primarily attributable to AI hype and venture capital rather than government resilience incentives (Kill Condition 5, partially met).

**The core finding is that "national resilience" functions as a semantic umbrella for reclassifying existing spending, not as a category of genuinely incremental capital.** Japan reaches 2% by relabeling Coast Guard and infrastructure. Germany counts interest payments on old military debt. NATO's 1.5% resilience category lacks any enforcement. The US reconciliation supplement is one-time money while the base budget is frozen and Biden-era industrial programs are being dismantled.

Two nuances prevent a complete kill. First, critical minerals represent a genuine — if small — pocket of new spending across all three countries, though execution failures (Lynas Texas, Iluka Eneabba cost overruns, declining JBIC disbursements) suggest the compounding assumption is premature. Second, European aggregate defense spending has increased in nominal terms (€218 billion in 2021 to €343 billion in 2024), with Poland's 4%+ spending unquestionably real. But the question was never whether some spending exists — it was whether spending is **genuinely incremental, structurally compounding, and durable across electoral cycles**. On those criteria, the weight of evidence tilts decisively toward skepticism.
