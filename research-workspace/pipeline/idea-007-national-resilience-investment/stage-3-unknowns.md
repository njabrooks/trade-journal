## Top 3 Unknowns (Detailed Analysis)

### Unknown 1: Is National Resilience Spending Genuinely Incremental?

**Decision Impact**: HIGH

This is the foundational question. Aitken describes spending that is "phenomenal at pace and at scale" across multiple countries. But governments routinely rebrand existing programs under fashionable headings. If "national resilience" is a new label on the same defense and infrastructure budgets that already existed, the thesis has no structural tailwind — it's narrative without incremental capital. Conversely, if we can identify net new appropriations, new bilateral funding mechanisms, and private sector CapEx responding to government incentives, the thesis has a verifiable economic foundation.

**Resolution Type**: empirical

**Externally Resolvable**: yes

Defense budgets, trade agreements, and government procurement contracts are public record. The Japan-US-Australia bilateral deals Aitken references should have verifiable funding commitments. The key is separating headline announcements from binding appropriations.

**Kill Condition**:
- Total government defense + infrastructure spending as % of GDP unchanged across US, Japan, Australia over the past 2 years despite "national resilience" rhetoric
- Takaichi-Trump bilateral agreement contains no binding funding commitments — memorandum of understanding only
- Japan's actual critical minerals funding disbursements (JOGMEC, JBIC) flat or declining vs. prior years
- US defense budget growth rate remains at CBO baseline projections (<3% nominal) through FY2027
- No identifiable private sector CapEx acceleration in resilience-adjacent sectors (defense tech, critical minerals processing)

**Conviction Increase Condition**:
- Verifiable net new appropriations: Japan's critical minerals budget increased >20% YoY; US defense tech budget shows above-trend growth
- Bilateral agreements with binding procurement contracts and multi-year funding schedules
- JOGMEC/JBIC disbursements to Australian critical minerals companies showing step-change increase
- Private sector CapEx announcements explicitly citing government resilience incentives (loan guarantees, procurement commitments)
- Multiple countries showing coordinated spending increases (not just one)

**Recommended Sources**:
- Japanese government budget documents (MOF): Defense and economic security line items, JOGMEC/JBIC annual reports
- US Congressional Research Service: Defense budget analysis, CHIPS Act / critical minerals appropriations tracking
- Australian government budget: Critical minerals strategy funding, fuel reserve investments
- NATO annual reports: Defense spending by member, commitment vs. actual
- Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI): Global defense spending database
- Company filings: Sumitomo Corp, Mitsubishi Corp, major defense contractors — CapEx guidance and government contract commentary

**Estimated Effort**: 8 hours

**Research Queries**:
1. What are Japan's actual FY2025 and FY2026 budget allocations for economic security, critical minerals, and defense — and how do they compare to FY2023/2024?
2. What binding commitments (not MOUs) emerged from the Takaichi-Trump Washington meeting? What are the dollar amounts and timelines?
3. What is the year-over-year change in JOGMEC and JBIC investments in Australian critical minerals projects specifically?
4. Has US defense spending growth accelerated beyond CBO baseline projections under the current administration?
5. Are there identifiable private sector CapEx announcements that cite government national resilience incentives as a catalyst?

### Unknown 2: Where in the Value Chain Does Margin Accrue?

**Decision Impact**: HIGH

Even if the spending is real and incremental, the thesis is only investable if public equity holders can capture the value. Government spending often flows through cost-plus contracts, sovereign-backed offtake agreements with capped margins, or directly to state-owned enterprises. Aitken names Sumitomo Corp and Sumitomo Electric as beneficiaries, but Japanese trading houses (sogo shosha) historically operate on thin margins as facilitators. The question is whether national resilience spending creates margin expansion for enabling companies or just revenue growth with compressed returns.

**Resolution Type**: industry

**Externally Resolvable**: yes

Trading house margins, defense contractor profitability, and mining company economics are reported in public filings. The structure of government contracts (cost-plus vs. fixed-price vs. milestone-based) is observable.

**Kill Condition**:
- Japanese trading houses show revenue growth from resilience-related segments but flat or declining operating margins
- Australian critical minerals companies funded by Japanese capital operate under restrictive offtake agreements that cap upside (fixed-price, long-term supply at below-market rates)
- Defense tech companies report margin compression despite revenue growth due to cost-plus contract structures
- Government procurement systematically favors national champions or state-owned enterprises over public equity investable companies
- Sumitomo Corp/Electric segment reporting shows no identifiable "national resilience" margin uplift vs. base business

**Conviction Increase Condition**:
- Trading houses demonstrate operating leverage on resilience-related revenue (margins expanding, not flat)
- Critical minerals companies secure market-rate or indexed-price offtake agreements with upside participation
- Defense tech companies operating on fixed-price contracts with margin expansion as scale increases
- Public equity beneficiaries identifiable across multiple value chain positions (not concentrated in one segment)
- Sumitomo Corp/Electric show margin expansion or re-rating in segments aligned with national resilience (infrastructure, metals, energy)

**Recommended Sources**:
- Sumitomo Corporation annual report and segment reporting: Mineral resources, infrastructure, metals segments
- Sumitomo Electric annual report: Automotive, electronics, environment & energy segments
- Mitsubishi Corporation segment reporting: For comparison across sogo shosha
- JOGMEC investment terms: Standard terms for sovereign-backed critical minerals investments
- Defense contractor filings (US: Palantir, Anduril predecessors; Europe: Rheinmetall, BAE; Japan: Mitsubishi Heavy): Contract structure commentary
- Australian Securities Exchange filings: Small-cap critical minerals companies with Japanese backing — look for offtake agreement terms

**Estimated Effort**: 6 hours

**Research Queries**:
1. What are Sumitomo Corp's operating margins by segment, and which segments are most exposed to national resilience themes (mineral resources, infrastructure, metals)?
2. What are the typical terms of JOGMEC/JBIC-backed investments in foreign critical minerals projects — do they cap upside or allow market-rate participation?
3. Which publicly listed Australian critical minerals companies have disclosed Japanese government or corporate backing, and what are their offtake terms?
4. How do sogo shosha margins on government-backed infrastructure projects compare to their base trading business?
5. In the US and European defense tech sector, are companies operating on cost-plus or fixed-price contracts, and what are the margin trends?

### Unknown 3: Do Crisis-Driven Spending Commitments Historically Persist?

**Decision Impact**: HIGH

The thesis claims national resilience spending will "accelerate through crises" and compound structurally. But the historical pattern for crisis-driven government spending is mixed. Post-9/11 defense spending persisted and grew for a decade. Post-2014 (Crimea) European defense commitments were largely unfulfilled until 2022. Post-COVID supply chain resilience rhetoric produced some reshoring but far less than announced. The question is which historical analogue best fits: durable commitment (post-9/11) or fading urgency (post-Crimea)?

**Resolution Type**: empirical + regulatory

**Externally Resolvable**: yes

Historical defense spending data, government commitment vs. actual disbursement rates, and the institutional mechanisms that lock in spending (treaties, multi-year appropriations, agency creation) are all researchable.

**Kill Condition**:
- Historical analysis shows that >60% of crisis-driven spending commitments in defense/infrastructure are walked back within 3 years of crisis resolution
- Post-Crimea NATO spending pledges: majority of members failed to reach 2% GDP target within 5 years (which is empirically true — need to assess whether the current moment is different)
- No institutional lock-in mechanisms identified (no new agencies created, no treaty-level commitments, no multi-year appropriations beyond current budget cycle)
- Japan's track record on prior "economic security" initiatives shows pattern of announcement without sustained follow-through

**Conviction Increase Condition**:
- Post-9/11 analogue holds: institutional mechanisms (DHS creation, AUMF, multi-year defense authorizations) locked in spending for 10+ years
- Current commitments include institutional lock-in: new agencies, treaty-level agreements, multi-year appropriations that survive budget cycles
- Japan's Economic Security Promotion Act (2022) has already created institutional infrastructure that Takaichi is accelerating, not starting from scratch
- Bipartisan support verified: opposition parties in US, Japan, Australia explicitly support national resilience spending
- Private sector co-investment creates self-reinforcing dynamic (companies that invest expect government to follow through, and lobby to ensure it)

**Recommended Sources**:
- SIPRI time-series: Post-crisis defense spending trajectories (post-9/11, post-Crimea, post-2022)
- NATO compliance reports: 2% GDP commitment vs. actual by member, trajectory analysis
- Japan's Economic Security Promotion Act (2022): Institutional structure, funding mechanisms, implementation progress
- US Congressional Budget Office: Multi-year defense authorization analysis, baseline vs. actual
- Academic literature: "Do crisis-driven fiscal commitments persist?" — political economy of defense spending
- RAND Corporation / Brookings: Analysis of spending commitment durability

**Estimated Effort**: 6 hours

**Research Queries**:
1. What is the historical track record of crisis-driven defense spending commitments — what % of announced increases are sustained beyond 3 years?
2. What institutional lock-in mechanisms exist for current national resilience commitments (new agencies, treaty obligations, multi-year appropriations)?
3. What is the implementation status of Japan's Economic Security Promotion Act (2022) — has it created durable institutional infrastructure or remained aspirational?
4. Is there bipartisan/multi-party support for national resilience spending in US, Japan, and Australia — specifically, do opposition parties support continuation?
5. What distinguishes post-9/11 spending durability from post-Crimea spending failure — which pattern does the current moment more closely resemble?
