# Nuclear restart capacity will fall far short of 8 GW by 2028

**The historical base rate for nuclear project timelines is brutal: projects consistently deliver 1.8-2.5x later than promised with 88-150% cost overruns.** Only three credible US restart candidates exist today—Palisades (800 MW), Three Mile Island Unit 1 (835 MW), and Duane Arnold (615 MW)—totaling just **2.25 GW of potential capacity**, with only the first two targeting pre-2029 completion. SMRs will contribute zero meaningful capacity before 2030 at the earliest. Japan's post-Fukushima restart program—the closest analogue—managed to restart only 14 of 54 reactors in 13 years, despite strong government support and $27 billion in safety investments. This analysis strongly supports the thesis that nuclear cannot meaningfully offset natural gas demand growth by 2028.

## Historical precedents reveal systematic timeline failure

The pattern is unmistakable: nuclear projects chronically underdeliver. **Watts Bar Unit 2**, the most relevant US completion precedent, took 9 years from restart announcement to commercial operation versus the 5 years TVA promised—an **80% schedule overrun** with **88% cost overrun** ($4.7 billion actual vs. $2.5 billion projected). TVA's CEO later admitted, "Our estimates on time and cost were wrong."

**Vogtle Units 3&4**, the benchmark for recent new construction, delivered 7+ years late (April 2016/2017 targets became July 2023/April 2024 actuals) with costs ballooning from **$14.3 billion to $36.8 billion—a 157% overrun**. The V.C. Summer project was abandoned entirely in 2017 after spending $9 billion of a revised $20-25 billion estimate, with executives later convicted of fraud.

The sole bright spot is **Browns Ferry Unit 1**, which completed its restart on time and on budget at $1.8 billion in 2007. Key differences: it was a restart/refurbishment rather than completion, had intact infrastructure, and used an experienced Bechtel workforce. This exception proves the rule—success required fundamentally different conditions than current restart candidates face.

| Project | Type | Predicted Timeline | Actual | Slippage | Cost Overrun |
|---------|------|-------------------|--------|----------|--------------|
| Watts Bar 2 | Completion | 5 years | 9 years | 1.8x | 88% |
| Vogtle 3&4 | New build | 4-5 years | 11-12 years | 2.5x | 157% |
| Browns Ferry 1 | Restart | 5 years | 5 years | 1.0x | ~0% |
| UK Hinkley Point C | New build | 2025 (2016 estimate) | 2029-2031 | 6-8 years | 72-94%+ |

Academic research confirms this isn't selection bias. A 2025 Boston University study found nuclear plants have the **highest construction cost overruns of any energy infrastructure type—averaging 102.5%** and **$1.56 billion more than expected**. MIT research documented labor productivity at Vogtle running **8-13x slower** than industry assumptions used for cost estimation.

## Japan's post-Fukushima program sets the international precedent

Japan provides the most instructive analogue for understanding nuclear restart dynamics at scale. After Fukushima in March 2011, all 54 of Japan's reactors progressively shut down, reaching **complete nuclear blackout by May 2012**. Industry initially expected relatively quick restarts after safety reviews—the government even briefly restarted Ohi Units 3&4 in July 2012 under provisional measures.

**The reality proved dramatically different.** The first permanent restart (Sendai-1) didn't occur until **August 2015—4.5 years after Fukushima**. As of January 2026, only **14-15 reactors have restarted out of 33 still classified as operable**—roughly 25% of pre-Fukushima capacity restored in 13+ years. Japan's nuclear electricity share plummeted from **30% pre-Fukushima to just 6-8% today**.

Industry forecasts consistently proved optimistic. The Institute of Energy Economics Japan predicted 7 reactors online by March 2017 and 19 by March 2018; actual figures were 4 and 9 respectively. Prime Minister Kishida's 2022 pledge of 9 units by winter 2022 and 7 more by summer 2023 also went unmet.

**Common causes of delay included:**
- New regulatory requirements requiring **$700 million to $4.5 billion per reactor** in safety upgrades
- Local government consent processes adding years of uncertainty
- Court injunctions repeatedly blocking approved restarts
- Anti-terrorism facility construction delays extending timelines into the 2030s
- BWRs facing greater challenges than PWRs (higher costs, more opposition)

The key lesson: **industry consistently underestimates restart timelines by 50-100%**, even with strong political will and massive financial commitment.

## Current US restart capacity tops out at 2.25 GW

Only three nuclear plants have credible restart plans, and their combined capacity falls dramatically short of the 8 GW threshold.

**Palisades (800 MW)** represents the most advanced restart attempt and achieved historic status in August 2025 as the first US plant to transition from decommissioning back to operational status. However, the restart has already slipped from "Q4 2025" to "early 2026" due to steam generator tube corrosion affecting over **12% of tubes**, requiring extensive sleeving repairs. NRC staff characterized the schedule as "very aggressive" and "very demanding." Total project support exceeds $3 billion including a $1.52 billion DOE loan guarantee.

**Three Mile Island Unit 1 (835 MW)**, now renamed Crane Clean Energy Center, targets 2027 restart backed by Microsoft's 20-year PPA valued at approximately $16 billion. Constellation received a $1 billion DOE loan in November 2025 and reports being ~80% staffed with 500 employees including ~130-150 returning former workers. The plant shut down in 2019 for economic reasons—importantly, this is the functional unit, not the damaged Unit 2 from the 1979 accident. Total investment: $1.6 billion.

**Duane Arnold (615 MW)** has the longest timeline, targeting Q1 2029 following NextEra's October 2025 deal with Google for a 25-year PPA. As a BWR with cooling tower damage from a 2020 derecho, it requires cooling tower reconstruction and different technical expertise than the PWR restarts.

**Indian Point (2,000 MW)** is speculative at best—Holtec floated the possibility in September 2025 but faces intense political opposition, and New York Governor Hochul stated there are no restart plans. Decommissioning is further advanced than other candidates.

|-------|----------|-------------|-------------------|
| Palisades | 800 MW | Early 2026 | High probability |
| Crane (TMI-1) | 835 MW | 2027 | Medium-high |
| Duane Arnold | 615 MW | Q1 2029 | No |
| **Total credible by 2028** | **1,635 MW** | | |
| **Total all candidates** | **2,250 MW** | | |

Industry analysts confirm this is the full universe. A ClearView Energy Partners analyst stated: "We're not convinced there are a lot of viable candidates." NextEra's CEO noted: "There are only a few nuclear plants that can be recommissioned in an economic way."

## SMRs will contribute zero capacity by 2028

The small modular reactor pathway is a dead end for 2028 capacity needs. **NuScale remains the only SMR with NRC design certification**, yet its flagship UAMPS Idaho project was cancelled in November 2023 after costs tripled from $2.7 billion (2015) to $9.3 billion, with projected power prices jumping 53% from $58/MWh to $89/MWh.

**TerraPower's Natrium project** in Wyoming—the most advanced US project—broke ground in June 2024 but only for non-nuclear construction. Nuclear island construction won't begin until **early 2027**, with realistic commercial operation in the **early 2030s**. The project was already delayed 2 years due to Russia's Ukraine invasion cutting off HALEU fuel supplies.

**X-energy's Dow Seadrift project** submitted its construction permit application in March 2025 with NRC granting an 18-month review timeline. Earliest commercial operation: **early 2030s**.

**Kairos Power's Hermes** demonstration reactor may produce ~20 MWe by 2027-2028, but this is a test project, not commercial scale.

The pattern of SMR timeline slippage is consistent across a decade of promises. DOE's 2020 ARDP program targeted demonstration reactors by 2028; that is now universally acknowledged as unachievable. IEEFA concluded in 2024-2025 that SMRs remain "too expensive, too slow to build, and too risky to play a significant role in transitioning from fossil fuels in the coming 10 to 15 years."

**Earliest meaningful SMR contribution (>100 MW commercial capacity): 2030-2032**, with commercial-scale deployment in the 2033-2035 timeframe.

## Hyperscaler PPAs change financing but not physics

Tech giants have fundamentally altered nuclear project economics through long-term power purchase agreements. Microsoft's 20-year PPA with Constellation for TMI at approximately **$110-115/MWh**—versus ~$80/MWh wholesale—provides revenue certainty that justifies restart investment and attracts DOE financing. Meta signed deals with Vistra (2,609 MW, 20 years) and Constellation (Clinton plant, 1,121 MW, 20 years). Amazon agreed to purchase 1,900 MW from Talen's Susquehanna plant through 2042.

These PPAs solve the financing problem that has historically plagued nuclear economics. The cost-per-MW comparison has shifted notably:

| Technology | Capital Cost/kW | Notes |
|------------|-----------------|-------|
| Nuclear restart | $1,900-2,250 | Uses existing infrastructure |
| New CCGT (2024) | $2,200-2,500 | Tripled from $722-875 in 2022 |
| New nuclear (US) | $6,000-10,000+ | Vogtle benchmark |
| SMR (projected) | ~$20,000/kW | NuScale cancellation estimate |

**Nuclear restarts are now cost-competitive with new gas plants** on a capital cost basis, and hyperscaler willingness to pay premium prices makes the economics work. However, PPAs cannot accelerate NRC review timelines, compress construction schedules, or eliminate technical risks from component degradation. The France 2022 corrosion crisis—which took 32 of 56 reactors offline simultaneously—demonstrates that even operating plants face multi-year outages from unforeseen issues.

## Thesis assessment strongly favors natural gas re-rating

The evidence overwhelmingly supports conviction increase in the natural gas re-rating thesis. Against the kill condition requiring **5+ restart projects with credible 2027-2028 timelines and >8 GW total capacity**, the reality offers only **2-3 projects with <2.3 GW maximum capacity**—falling short by nearly 75%.

**Kill condition status: NOT MET**
- ❌ 5+ restart projects: Only 3 credible candidates exist
- ❌ >8 GW capacity: Maximum 2.25 GW, only 1.6 GW by 2028
- ❌ NRC fast-tracks: Maintaining 2-4 year timelines; Palisades already slipping
- ❌ SMRs achieve commercial operation: Zero commercial capacity before 2030

**Conviction increase indicators: ALL MET**
- ✅ Restarts limited to 2-3 projects: Exactly 3 credible candidates
- ✅ <4 GW capacity: 2.25 GW total, 1.6 GW by 2028
- ✅ NRC maintains cautious timelines: 2-4 years for restart approvals
- ✅ Historical pattern of cost/schedule overruns: 80-157% documented
- ✅ SMRs remain demonstration-phase: Earliest commercial 2030+

The historical base rate for nuclear delivering on schedule is approximately **10-20%**. Typical timeline slippage runs **1.8-2.5x** original estimates. Even under optimistic scenarios where Palisades and TMI both achieve 2027-2028 operation, total nuclear additions would represent **<1.7 GW against 5x acceleration in electricity demand growth**. Nuclear restarts will not offset meaningful gas demand—they represent a rounding error in the capacity gap thesis.

## Conclusion

Nuclear capacity additions by 2028 face insurmountable constraints: only three viable restart candidates totaling 2.25 GW exist, historical precedents show 80-157% schedule overruns, Japan's 13-year restart program restored only 25% of pre-shutdown capacity, and SMRs remain 4-6 years from commercial contribution. Hyperscaler PPAs solve financing but cannot accelerate construction physics or regulatory timelines. The realistic nuclear contribution by 2028—approximately **1.6 GW** if both Palisades and TMI succeed without further delays—represents less than 20% of the 8 GW threshold and cannot meaningfully compete with natural gas for meeting accelerating electricity demand growth. The thesis that US natural gas prices will structurally re-rate higher by 2028 gains conviction from this analysis.
