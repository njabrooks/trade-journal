# US natural gas supply cannot respond fast enough—thesis validated

**The investment thesis that US natural gas prices will structurally re-rate higher by 2028 is supported by supply-side evidence.** At $4-5/MMBTU price levels, US shale gas faces binding constraints across four dimensions: accelerating basin decline rates, structural labor and equipment shortages, persistent pipeline bottlenecks in Appalachia, and deeply embedded producer capital discipline. The industry can likely deliver only **5-10 BCF/day of incremental supply within 12 months**—far short of the 20+ BCF/day that would be needed to invalidate this thesis. The 2022 price spike, when production grew just 4% despite $9+ prices, demonstrated that supply elasticity has fundamentally declined in the capital discipline era.

## Basin decline rates have become the treadmill that can't be outrun

The critical mechanism supporting higher prices is deteriorating well quality combined with steep inherent decline curves. **Haynesville wells drilled in 2022 produced 31% less estimated ultimate recovery (EUR) than 2019 vintage wells**, while 2021 wells showed a 17% EUR decline versus 2019. This isn't a cyclical phenomenon—it reflects systematic degradation as operators exhaust their highest-quality drilling locations. Combined with first-year decline rates of **60-70%** for new shale wells, the Marcellus alone requires approximately **900-1,000 new wells annually** just to maintain flat production, according to [EIA](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=48537) data and industry analysis.

Current production gaps to recent peaks are substantial but not easily restored. Haynesville output has fallen from **14.6 BCF/day in 2023 to approximately 11 BCF/day in early 2025**—a 24% decline driven by deliberate producer curtailments amid low prices. The Utica Shale has dropped **33% from its December 2019 peak** of 8.3 BCF/day to just 5.6 BCF/day. These represent theoretical restoration potential, but realizing it requires sustained capital deployment that producers have explicitly rejected. Aethon Energy, a major Haynesville producer running 8 rigs versus a prior peak of 16, has stated it needs **sustained $5/MMBTU prices to significantly ramp drilling**, per [Natural Gas Intelligence](https://www.naturalgasintel.com/news/questions-mount-for-haynesville-natural-gas-supply-as-producers-play-game-of-rig-chicken/).

The drilled-but-uncompleted (DUC) well inventory—historically a buffer allowing rapid supply response—has been depleted. Combined Appalachia and Haynesville DUCs stand at approximately **1,615 wells as of April 2024**, near historic lows and insufficient to bridge any meaningful demand acceleration. [Enverus](https://www.enverus.com/newsroom/duc-hunt-what-the-2025-drawdown-means/) warns that operators are "entering 2025 with lower DUC inventory than 2024," eliminating the quick-response mechanism that existed in prior cycles.

## Labor and equipment constraints create hard ceilings on activity growth

The US oilfield services industry has structurally downsized while achieving record production through efficiency gains—creating a paradox where rapid scaling is now nearly impossible. **The oil and gas extraction workforce stands at 141,000**, down from 152,000 in 2022 and a peak of over 200,000 in late 2014. The industry faces a projected shortage of **40,000 skilled professionals** including geoscientists, drilling engineers, and completion specialists, according to [industry analysis](https://www.learntodrill.com/post/labor-shortage-in-oil-gas). Critically, experienced workers who left during the 2020 downturn have found employment in other industries and "will not return."

Equipment availability imposes equally binding constraints. Active frac spreads have declined to **156 in January 2026**, down 39 from the prior year. The estimated constraint ceiling before hitting equipment bottlenecks is **310-320 spreads**—roughly double current levels but requiring 6-12 month lead times to procure. Patterson-UTI is **retiring approximately 400,000 horsepower of Tier-2 diesel frac equipment** without replacement, while CEO Andy Hendricks notes that future demand "is going to be on equipment that can burn natural gas... Tier 2 is not going to have an easy place." Only 8% of frac spreads are fully electric, and the technology transition is consuming capital that might otherwise expand capacity.

Service company behavior confirms these constraints are binding. Halliburton announced in Q3 2025 that it "idled equipment that no longer meets our return expectations," while Liberty Energy CEO Ron Gusek stated that "oil and gas industry frac activity has now fallen below levels required to sustain North American oil production." Liberty projects **industry recovery by mid-2026**—an 18+ month timeline that rules out rapid response. Gas-directed rig counts at **124-127 rigs** represent just **8% of the 2008 peak of 1,585 rigs**, with realistic growth capped at 10-20% annually given current constraints.

## Appalachian pipeline bottlenecks strand the cheapest gas from markets

Marcellus/Utica production of **35-37 BCF/day** consistently exceeds effective takeaway capacity to out-of-basin markets, creating structural price discounts. Eastern Gas South (formerly Dominion South) trades at an average discount of **$0.80/MMBTU below Henry Hub**—a direct measure of the infrastructure constraint. The [EIA](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=49377) estimated Northeast-to-other-regions pipeline capacity at approximately **24.5 BCF/day** as of 2021, with many existing pipelines operating at or near **100% utilization** on peak days.

The **Mountain Valley Pipeline** (MVP) coming online in June 2024 added **2.0 BCF/day** of capacity—the first major Appalachian pipeline completion since 2018. The project took **10 years from proposal to commissioning** and cost **$7.2 billion versus an original estimate of $3.5 billion**. This timeline illustrates the regulatory gauntlet facing new infrastructure: typical greenfield interstate pipelines require 5-10+ years with high litigation risk, while even brownfield compression expansions take 2-4 years.

The pipeline project queue offers only gradual relief through 2030:

- **MVP Boost** (0.6 BCF/d) - FERC application filed July 2025, targeting 2028 in-service
- **Borealis Pipeline** (2.0 BCF/d) - FID expected Q1 2026, completion potentially 2029-2030  
- **Transco SESE** (~1.0 BCF/d) - FERC application filed, targeting late 2027
- **MVP Southgate** (0.55 BCF/d) - permitting ongoing, mid-2028 target

Total proposed incremental capacity of **5.7-6.75 BCF/day beyond current additions** is theoretically substantial but faces significant execution risk. Recent regulatory changes including FERC's waiver of Order 871 may accelerate some projects, but the [historical record](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=62323)—Atlantic Coast Pipeline canceled after $8 billion spent, PennEast canceled, Constitution canceled—suggests caution about assuming full build-out.

## The 2022 price spike revealed permanent changes in supply elasticity

The most powerful validation of structurally higher prices comes from producer behavior during the 2022 price spike. Despite Henry Hub prices reaching **$9.85/MMBTU in August 2022** and averaging **$6.45/MMBTU for the year** (highest since 2008), US dry gas production grew only **4% annually**—a modest response that would have been unthinkable during the 2009-2019 shale boom when growth rates routinely exceeded 10-15%.

The [Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas Energy Survey](https://www.dallasfed.org/research/surveys/des/2022/2201) from Q1 2022 asked executives why publicly traded producers were restraining growth despite elevated prices. The results were unambiguous: **59% cited investor pressure to maintain capital discipline**, 15% cited other factors, 11% cited ESG concerns, 8% cited insufficient workforce, and only 6% cited government regulations. Pioneer Natural Resources CEO Scott Sheffield captured the sentiment: "Whether it's $150 oil, $200 oil or $100 oil, we're not going to change our growth plans."

This capital discipline remains firmly embedded in 2025-2026 producer guidance:

- **EQT Corporation** (largest US producer): Maintaining a **$2.00/MMBTU unlevered FCF breakeven** while targeting debt reduction to $5 billion by YE2026—explicitly not pursuing growth
- **Expand Energy** (Chesapeake + Southwestern): Guiding to modest **6% production growth** in 2026 while prioritizing $500 million in synergies and maintaining investment-grade rating
- **Antero Resources**: Running just **2 drilling rigs** with flat production guidance of 3.4-3.45 BCF/day, using FCF to pay down 30% of total debt
- **Comstock Resources**: Increasing rigs from 5 to 7 but focused on Western Haynesville delineation, not volume growth

Only **Range Resources explicitly targets meaningful growth**—approximately 20% to 2.6 BCF/day by 2027—but even this represents measured expansion rather than boom-time acceleration. Breakeven costs of **$2.00-$3.50/MMBTU** across major basins confirm that producers could profitably expand at current $3-4 prices, but they are choosing not to do so.

## Remaining uncertainties and counterarguments

The thesis faces legitimate challenges. First, **Permian associated gas continues growing** as operators drill for oil—production rose 10% (1.6 BCF/day) in 2024 alone, partially offsetting gas basin declines. Second, **service costs have begun falling** (down 10% in 2024 per Goldman Sachs estimates), which could improve drilling economics and encourage modest activity increases. Third, **recent FERC regulatory changes** including the Order 871 waiver may accelerate pipeline permitting timelines, though the historical record suggests skepticism is warranted.

The most significant risk to the thesis is **producer resolve weakening** under sustained $4-5 prices. While capital discipline has held through multiple cycles, some operators may eventually shift strategy—particularly private equity-backed producers less beholden to public market pressure. The Expand Energy CFO noted: "Supply is going to be flat to down and probably remain that way until you see a rig count change—which then has quite a lag time to it." This lag time—estimated at **4-6 months** from price signal to rig response per the [EIA](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=60782)—plus an additional 6-12 months to bring new wells online, provides structural protection for the higher-price thesis even if discipline eventually breaks.

## Conclusion

The supply-side evidence **strongly supports** the thesis that US natural gas prices will structurally re-rate higher by 2028. The industry faces a fundamentally different supply elasticity regime than the 2009-2019 shale boom: capital discipline is institutionally embedded, the workforce has structurally downsized, equipment is being retired rather than expanded, pipeline bottlenecks persist in the largest producing basin, and well quality continues deteriorating. At $4-5/MMBTU prices, realistic incremental supply capacity of 5-10 BCF/day over 12 months is insufficient to meet a demand acceleration scenario without sustained higher prices. The 2022 price spike—when supply grew only 4% despite $9+ prices—demonstrated that this is not theoretical constraint but revealed behavior. Unless demand growth materially disappoints or a new source of supply emerges, the structural case for higher natural gas prices through 2028 remains intact.
