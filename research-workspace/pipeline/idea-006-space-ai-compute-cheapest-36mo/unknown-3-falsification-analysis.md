# Unknown 3 Falsification: Starship Reuse Cadence and Achieved $/kg

**Research Date**: 2026-02-10
**Objective**: Find evidence that this thesis is WRONG

## Findings

### Finding 1: SpaceX achieved only 5 Starship flights in 2025 — 80% below its own 25-flight target
- **Source**: company communications / industry reporting
- **Source URL/Reference**: [Wikipedia — List of Starship flights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Starship_and_Super_Heavy_launches); [Space.com — Kathy Lueders 25-flight target](https://www.space.com/spacex-starship-launch-plans-2025)
- **Credibility**: high — cross-referenced flight manifest data from multiple outlets including NASASpaceflight.com
- **Content**: Starbase General Manager Kathy Lueders stated in November 2024 that SpaceX targeted 25 Starship missions in 2025. Through mid-October 2025, only **5 flights launched** (Flights 7–11), with no planned launches for the remainder of the year. The total Starship manifest stands at 11 integrated flights since April 2023, of which **5 were failures** — a 45% failure rate. No flights occurred between October 2025 and February 2026, as SpaceX transitions to the Block 3 / V3 vehicle.
- **Bearing on thesis**: Directly supports the kill condition. A vehicle achieving ~4 flights/year average cannot reach the cadence needed for low $/kg. SpaceX's demonstrated ability to predict its own near-term flight rate is poor, undermining confidence in any forward projections. The 80% miss on a one-year target suggests 100+ flights/year by 2028 is unrealistic by a wide margin.

### Finding 2: No Starship upper stage has ever been recovered — ship reuse remains entirely undemonstrated
- **Source**: company communications / industry reporting
- **Source URL/Reference**: [NASASpaceflight.com — Starship flight history](https://www.nasaspaceflight.com/); [Space.com — TPS challenges](https://www.space.com/spacex-starship-heat-shield-biggest-challenge)
- **Credibility**: high — factual record from multiple space journalism outlets
- **Content**: As of February 2026, **zero Starship upper stages have been recovered intact**. No ship has been caught by the tower. No ship has been reflown. Of the 6 Block 2 ships that attempted flight in 2025, 4 were lost (Ships 33, 34, 35, 36 — each with a different failure mode). Ships 37 and 38 completed successful reentry and ocean splashdown but both sank — they were never designed for recovery. Ship catch is now expected with Block 3 vehicles sometime in 2026 at the earliest. Musk himself called the reusable heat shield "the single biggest engineering challenge" and "the biggest remaining problem" in May 2024. SpaceX is still iterating on TPS design — testing "crunch wrap" felt linings, ablative secondary layers, and metallic tile alternatives.
- **Bearing on thesis**: This is the single most critical falsification finding. **Upper stage reuse is the key to sub-$200/kg economics.** Without ship recovery, every flight expends a ~$50–100M upper stage. The thesis requires rapid, reliable ship recovery and refly — a capability that does not exist and has never been demonstrated even once. The 36-month clock is ticking against a technology that hasn't reached Step 1 (recovery) of a multi-step maturation process (recovery → inspection → refurbishment → refly → rapid turnaround).

### Finding 3: Booster reuse turnaround demonstrated at 4.5 months — not hours
- **Source**: industry reporting
- **Source URL/Reference**: [Orbital Today — Booster 14 refly](https://orbitaltoday.com/); [Space Explored — Falcon 9 turnaround record](https://spaceexplored.com/)
- **Credibility**: high — based on observable flight dates and refurbishment reporting
- **Content**: The only booster refly was Booster 14, which flew on Flight 7 (January 16, 2025) and reflew on Flight 9 (May 27, 2025) — a **calendar gap of 4.5 months**. Orbital Today described the refurbishment portion as "under three months." On its second flight, Booster 14 was lost during the landing burn, likely due to a Raptor engine explosion, meaning **no booster has been caught, reflown, and caught again**. For comparison, Falcon 9's fastest-ever booster turnaround is 9 days (achieved March 2025), after nearly a decade of optimization on a far simpler vehicle with 9 engines instead of 33. The Space Shuttle's record turnaround was 54 days. SpaceX's aspirational "under an hour" turnaround and "multiple launches per day" from the same pad remain pure aspiration.
- **Bearing on thesis**: Strongly supports the kill condition. Even optimistic extrapolation from a 4.5-month demonstrated turnaround does not reach the 72-hour turnaround the conviction-increase condition requires by end of 2026. The gap between demonstrated and required performance is roughly **60x** (4.5 months ÷ 72 hours).

### Finding 4: V3/Block 3 booster failed ground pressure testing — the "production vehicle" is resetting the development clock
- **Source**: industry reporting / company communications
- **Source URL/Reference**: [SpaceNews — Booster 18 anomaly](https://spacenews.com/); [Teslarati — B18 failure](https://www.teslarati.com/); [Space.com — V3 testing](https://www.space.com/)
- **Credibility**: high — observed anomaly confirmed by multiple outlets and photographic evidence
- **Content**: Booster 18, the first V3 Super Heavy booster, suffered a catastrophic structural failure during gas system pressure testing on November 21, 2025. The LOX tank section crumpled "like an empty soda can" before any propellant was loaded or engines installed. SpaceX VP of Launch Kiko Dontchev had described V3 as the "production rocket" just 16 days earlier at the Economist Space Summit. SpaceX pivoted to Booster 19, stacked by late December 2025, but Flight 12 slipped to early 2026. V3 incorporates major changes: integrated hot-stage ring, Raptor 3 engines (which have never flown), 3 grid fins instead of 4, and redesigned propellant systems. Block 2 was retired after 5 flights without ever reaching orbit, with a final reusable payload estimate of only **35 tonnes** (vs. 100–200t design goal). Each block transition effectively resets the testing and reliability clock.
- **Bearing on thesis**: Supports the kill condition. The thesis requires a mature, production vehicle with demonstrated reuse by 2027–2028. As of February 2026, the "production" vehicle has never flown, its first booster failed ground testing, and its engines (Raptor 3) are untested in flight. This pushes realistic high-cadence operations well beyond the 36-month thesis window.

### Finding 5: FAA caps Boca Chica at 25 launches/year — no path to higher rates exists
- **Source**: FAA regulatory filing
- **Source URL/Reference**: [FAA Starship Stakeholder Engagement page](https://www.faa.gov/space/stakeholder_engagement/spacex_starship); [FAA Final Tiered EA (May 2025)](https://www.faa.gov/media/94346)
- **Credibility**: high — official government regulatory documents
- **Content**: The FAA's May 2025 Tiered Environmental Assessment and FONSI/ROD capped Boca Chica at **25 orbital launches, 25 Super Heavy landings, and 25 Starship landings per year**. This followed a lengthy review with two public comment periods receiving 12,303 comments. No environmental review has been initiated for exceeding 25 launches/year from Boca Chica. Exceeding this cap would likely require a full Environmental Impact Statement (EIS), which typically takes 1–2+ years. While the FAA separately approved up to 44 launches/year from LC-39A (EIS finalized ~January 2026) and 76 launches/year from SLC-37 (EIS signed November 2025), neither Florida pad is operational for Starship yet.
- **Bearing on thesis**: Partially supports the kill condition. The 25/year Boca Chica cap is half of the 50/year kill threshold. However, the regulatory ceiling across all approved sites totals ~145/year — meaning the kill condition's specific "50/year from Boca Chica" limit is already triggered, but SpaceX could theoretically meet the spirit of the requirement across multiple sites. The practical constraint is that only Boca Chica is currently operational, and even there SpaceX achieved only 5 of the 25 allowed flights.

### Finding 6: Cape Canaveral infrastructure is years behind — LC-39A late 2026 at earliest, SLC-37 not before 2028
- **Source**: industry reporting / regulatory filings
- **Source URL/Reference**: [NASASpaceflight.com — LC-39A construction updates](https://www.nasaspaceflight.com/); [Federal Register — SLC-37 EIS ROD (Nov 2025)](https://www.federalregister.gov/)
- **Credibility**: high — based on observable construction progress and official filings
- **Content**: LC-39A's Starship launch tower was completed in September 2022 but sat unused for years. The **original launch mount was scrapped in early 2025** in favor of a completely new design — a significant timeline setback. The new launch mount was transported to the pad in November 2025. Flame trench construction, chopstick installation, and tank farm work remain ongoing. NASASpaceflight.com estimates first Starship launch from LC-39A "potentially in late 2026." For SLC-37, the EIS was signed November 2025, but **construction has not yet started** on Starship infrastructure — SpaceX doesn't even have a lease agreement yet. Two launch towers, tank farms, a methane liquefier, air separation unit, and deluge systems must all be built from scratch. Earliest operational date: late 2027 to 2028. SpaceX's Roberts Road production facility (Giga Bay) in Florida is expected complete only by end of 2026.
- **Bearing on thesis**: Strongly supports the kill condition for the 36-month window. Through at least late 2026, Starship can launch only from Boca Chica (capped at 25/year). LC-39A adds 44/year potential but likely not until 2027 in practical terms. SLC-37's 76/year won't contribute until 2028+. The realistic combined operational capacity through end-2027 is ~25–30 flights/year.

### Finding 7: Falcon 9 took 12 years to reach 50+ flights/year — the most relevant historical precedent
- **Source**: company data / industry analysis
- **Source URL/Reference**: [ElonX.net — Falcon 9 launch history](https://www.elonx.net/); [Wikipedia — List of Falcon 9 launches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Falcon_9_and_Falcon_Heavy_launches)
- **Credibility**: high — based on complete public flight manifest
- **Content**: Falcon 9's first flight was June 2010. It first exceeded 50 flights in 2022 (12 years later) and 100 flights in 2024 (14 years later). The progression: 2 flights in 2010, 6 in 2014, 18 in 2017, 61 in 2022, 134 in 2024, 165 in 2025. Falcon 9 Block 5 — the reuse-optimized version — didn't fly until May 2018, 8 years after the first flight. First booster to reach 10 reflights: May 2021 (5.5 years after first landing). First to reach 20: April 2024 (8.3 years). First to reach 30: August 2025 (9.7 years). Falcon 9 is far simpler than Starship: 1 engine on the upper stage (vs. 6), 9 on the booster (vs. 33), single reusable element (vs. both stages), proven kerosene propulsion, and existing launch infrastructure.
- **Bearing on thesis**: Devastating to the thesis timeline. If the simpler Falcon 9 took 12 years to reach 50 flights/year, expecting Starship to do it in ~5 years from first flight (2023→2028) contradicts all historical precedent. The thesis requires airline-like operations by 2028–2029 for a vehicle that, as of early 2026, has never reached orbit, never recovered its upper stage, and just failed a ground test on its latest booster variant.

### Finding 8: Space Shuttle promised 60 flights/year at $5.5M — delivered 4.5/year at $1.6B per flight
- **Source**: academic / NASA historical analysis
- **Source URL/Reference**: [Space.com — Shuttle program costs](https://www.space.com/12166-space-shuttle-program-cost-promises-209-billion.html); [NASA Technical Reports — Jones (2018)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200001093/downloads/20200001093.pdf)
- **Credibility**: high — official NASA cost data and peer-reviewed analysis
- **Content**: The 1973 Mathematica economic analysis projected 514 Shuttle flights over 12 years (avg. 43/year). NASA promised flight costs of $5.5–9M ($20M adjusted). Actual performance: **135 flights over 30 years (4.5/year avg., peak 9/year), at $1.6B per flight** (total program cost $209B). Even the incremental marginal cost was $409M per flight. A 2018 NASA paper by Harry Jones concluded: "Surprisingly, launch vehicle reuse — the most anticipated method to cut cost — has not so far actually cut cost and probably contributed to very high shuttle launch cost." TPS tile maintenance required **17,000 hours between flights** and "nearly two man-years of work for every flight" to glue on 24,300 tiles.
- **Bearing on thesis**: The strongest historical analogue. The Shuttle demonstrates how reusable vehicles with tile-based thermal protection can structurally fail to deliver on cost and cadence promises by orders of magnitude. Starship uses mechanically attached tiles (better than glue) but faces similar fundamental materials challenges. The pattern of overpromising reuse economics and underdelivering is deeply embedded in spaceflight history. A NASA paper finding that "reuse has not so far actually cut cost" should give any investor pause.

### Finding 9: Independent analysts estimate Starship pricing at $1,000–2,500/kg — 5–12x above the thesis threshold
- **Source**: independent industry analysis
- **Source URL/Reference**: [SpaceDotBiz — Starship pricing analysis](https://newsletter.spacedotbiz.com/p/starship-really-going-revolutionize-launch-costs); [Longshot Space — SpaceX market analysis](https://www.longshotspace.com/post/spacex-and-the-market-for-launch)
- **Credibility**: medium-high — independent analysts with space industry expertise but some are competitors
- **Content**: SpaceDotBiz analyst Ian Vorbach predicts Starship will likely be **priced at $150M–$250M per launch** initially, yielding $1,500–$2,500/kg — "not the order of magnitude or greater price reduction that I see many hoping for." Longshot Space analysis concludes: "For the next ~20 years, it's going to cost a bit less than the Falcon 9 per kg, perhaps as little as half ($2,500 to $1,000/kg)... I would believe $500/kg... but I'm not believing $100/kg, much less $10/kg. It may be the case that $10/kg is simply not in the cards ever." Even optimistic NextBigFuture projections show sub-$100/kg requires 6+ flights per vehicle with 200-tonne payloads. The critical issue: **Falcon 9 prices actually increased from $61.2M to $67–70M after reuse was demonstrated** — SpaceX captured reuse savings as margin rather than passing them to customers.
- **Bearing on thesis**: Strongly supports falsification. The thesis requires sub-$200/kg. The most credible independent estimates cluster at $1,000–2,500/kg for the foreseeable future. Even SpaceX's internal cost may be $500–1,000/kg initially. The Falcon 9 precedent — where reuse savings were retained as margin, not passed through — suggests SpaceX will price Starship to maximize revenue, not to enable a speculative space compute market.

### Finding 10: Raptor engine failures caused multiple vehicle losses — 33 engines per booster creates compounding risk
- **Source**: company data / industry reporting
- **Source URL/Reference**: [Wikipedia — SpaceX Raptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Raptor); [Space Connect — Raptor 3 status](https://www.spaceconnectonline.com.au/)
- **Credibility**: high — based on observed flight anomalies documented by multiple sources
- **Content**: During Raptor development, "more than 50 chambers melted, and more than 20 engines exploded." In 2021, Musk warned of a "Raptor production crisis" threatening SpaceX bankruptcy. In 2025 flights: Flight 7's ship was lost to harmonic vibrations in the engine section; Flight 8 lost its ship to "inadvertent propellant mixing and ignition" from a Raptor hardware failure; Flight 9's booster was likely lost to a Raptor explosion during landing burn; Flight 11 had one Raptor fail to relight during boost-back. With **33 engines per booster and 6 per ship (39 total)**, even 99% individual engine reliability yields only ~67% probability of zero failures per flight. The new Raptor 3 engine has never flown. Over 300 ground tests have been conducted, but ground testing and flight performance historically diverge, especially for full-flow staged combustion engines operating at 300+ bar.
- **Bearing on thesis**: Supports the kill condition. Engine reliability is a fundamental constraint on reuse economics. Each engine failure risks vehicle loss, which resets reuse counts to zero. The statistical challenge of 39 engines per stack means even excellent per-engine reliability translates to meaningful per-flight risk. This creates a structural floor on loss rates that degrades reuse economics — per a 2016 NASA paper, even a 5% vehicle attrition rate caps effective vehicle life at 20 flights regardless of design life.

- **Content**: Former NASA flight director Paul Hill, speaking for the Aerospace Safety Advisory Panel (ASAP) in September 2025 after visiting Starbase, stated: **"The HLS schedule is significantly challenged and [in] our estimation could be years late for a 2027 Artemis III Moon landing."** He identified specific threats: timely development of Starship V3, reliable flight demonstration of tanker/depot configurations, and Raptor 3 performance/reliability. - **Bearing on thesis**: While the HLS timeline isn't directly about commercial launch costs, it serves as a credibility test for SpaceX's Starship development predictions. If NASA's own independent safety assessors believe Starship development is "years late" and "significantly challenged," this strongly suggests the vehicle will not mature to rapid, routine operations within the thesis's 36-month window.

### Finding 12: Block 2 Starship reusable payload was only 35 tonnes — far below the 100–200t needed for low $/kg
- **Source**: company data / industry analysis
- **Source URL/Reference**: [Payload Research — Starship expendable configuration](https://payloadspace.com/payload-research-starships-progress-and-exploring-expendable-configuration/); [NextBigFuture — Starship cost roadmap](https://www.nextbigfuture.com/2025/01/spacex-starship-roadmap-to-100-times-lower-cost-launch.html)
- **Credibility**: medium-high — industry analysis based on observed vehicle performance
- **Content**: Block 2 Starship was retired with a final estimated reusable payload to orbit of only **35 tonnes** — far below SpaceX's 100–200 tonne design goal. Even at a theoretical $2M marginal cost, 35t payload yields **$57/kg** — but this assumes vehicle reuse that doesn't exist. At realistic early-operations costs of $50–100M per expendable flight, 35t yields $1,400–2,900/kg. Block 3 / V3 targets higher payload but has never flown. The $/kg math is extraordinarily sensitive to actual payload mass: if the operational reusable payload settles at 50–100t instead of 200t, every $/kg estimate doubles to quadruples. NextBigFuture's optimistic projections assume 200t payload — an aspirational figure no Starship variant has demonstrated.
- **Bearing on thesis**: Critically undermines the thesis. Sub-$200/kg requires BOTH low per-flight cost AND high payload mass. Even if marginal cost targets are met, a 50t real-world payload (vs. 200t theoretical) would push $/kg to 4x the projected level — from $50/kg to $200/kg under optimistic assumptions, or from $500/kg to $2,000/kg under realistic ones.

## Kill Condition Assessment

**Kill condition from thesis**:
> By end of 2027 (18 months from now, halfway through the 36-month thesis window): Starship has not achieved booster reuse, OR achieved reuse cadence is under 10 flights per booster, OR FAA has imposed flight rate caps below 50/year from Boca Chica. Any of these would push the $/kg well above the threshold needed for space compute economics to work within the thesis timeframe.

**Evidence FOR kill condition being triggered**:
- **FAA cap already triggered**: Boca Chica is capped at 25 launches/year — half the 50/year threshold. No environmental review for higher rates has been initiated.
- **Booster reuse cadence nowhere near 10 flights**: The best-case booster has flown twice (Booster 14 and Booster 15, each reflown once). Booster 14 was lost on its second flight. No booster has achieved even 3 flights, let alone 10. Achieving 10 flights per booster by end of 2027 would require unprecedented acceleration from the current demonstrated pace.
- **Ship reuse at zero**: No ship has ever been recovered. This is the binding constraint on $/kg, and there is no evidence it will be solved within 18 months.
- **Flight cadence of ~4/year vs. 50+ needed**: Actual demonstrated cadence (11 flights over ~30 months) averages less than 5/year.
- **V3 development still in early testing**: The "production vehicle" has never flown, and its first booster failed ground testing in November 2025.
- **Falcon 9 precedent**: 12 years from first flight to 50+ flights/year. Starship is in year 3 with a far more complex vehicle.

**Evidence AGAINST kill condition being triggered**:
- **Booster reuse exists**: SpaceX has reflown two boosters (Flights 9 and 11), demonstrating the basic concept works. The catch mechanism has succeeded 3 times.
- **Regulatory ceiling is high across sites**: Combined approved capacity is 145/year (25 Boca Chica + 44 LC-39A + 76 SLC-37). The Boca Chica cap is a site-specific limit, not a system-wide one.
- **SpaceX's iterative pace is accelerating**: Despite 2025's setbacks, each flight has yielded engineering data. Block 3 / V3 incorporates lessons from 11 flights.
- **Political environment favors SpaceX**: The Trump administration's August 2025 executive order directs agencies to reduce regulatory barriers for commercial space. FAA now grants return-to-flight before investigation closure.
- **Manufacturing capacity is expanding**: Starfactory and Florida Giga Bay are under construction. Raptor 3 is designed for faster, cheaper production.
- **Falcon 9 analogues show eventual success**: Falcon 9 ultimately reached 165 flights in 2025 with 30+ reuses per booster. The question is timing, not feasibility.

**Assessment**: **PARTIALLY TRIGGERED**

The FAA flight rate cap component is **already triggered** — Boca Chica is capped at 25/year, well below 50. The booster reuse cadence component is **on track to trigger** — no booster has achieved even 3 flights, making 10 flights per booster by end-2027 extremely unlikely. The booster reuse existence component is **not triggered** — booster reuse has been demonstrated (twice), though both reflown boosters were subsequently lost. The overall kill condition is met on 2 of 3 criteria and trending toward the third.

**Confidence in assessment**: **high** — The evidence is based on observable flight history, official FAA regulatory documents, and SpaceX's own demonstrated performance versus stated targets. The factual record is clear; the only uncertainty is the pace of future improvement, which would need to be historically unprecedented to avoid triggering the kill condition.

## Caveats and Limitations
- **SpaceX is a private company**: Internal cost data, refurbishment timelines, and engineering assessments are not publicly available. All cost estimates rely on external modeling with significant assumptions.
- **V3/Block 3 represents a step change**: Block 3 may perform dramatically better than Block 2. The transition to Raptor 3 and structural redesigns could accelerate the reuse timeline in ways not predictable from Block 2 data. However, Block 3 has not yet flown, making this speculative.
- **Musk's political connections**: The current administration's close relationship with Musk could accelerate regulatory approvals in ways that standard timelines don't capture. The August 2025 executive order on space launch regulation is evidence of this.
- **No access to SpaceX internal documents**: The Politico leak (November 2025) provided some internal timeline data, but SpaceX's actual cost-per-flight data, refurbishment cost data, and engineering confidence levels remain proprietary.
- **Falcon 9 is an imperfect analogue**: Starship benefits from SpaceX's accumulated institutional knowledge from Falcon 9 reuse, which could accelerate the learning curve. However, Starship's greater complexity (fully reusable, TPS, 39 engines, catch mechanism, cryogenic upper stage) works against this.
- **Selection bias in sources**: Space journalism sources (NASASpaceflight, Ars Technica) tend to be technically rigorous but may skew toward enthusiast optimism. Skeptical analyst sources may underweight SpaceX's demonstrated ability to eventually deliver on ambitious goals (albeit years late).

## Summary

The evidence strongly supports falsification of the thesis's Starship assumptions within the 36-month window. **The kill condition is partially triggered today and on trajectory to be fully triggered by end-2027.** The FAA has already capped Boca Chica at 25 flights/year — half the thesis threshold — with no review initiated for higher rates. No booster has flown more than twice, and both reflown boosters were subsequently lost, making 10 flights per booster by end-2027 extraordinarily unlikely. Most critically, no Starship upper stage has ever been recovered, let alone reflown. Without ship reuse, the $/kg economics cannot work at any flight rate. The entire Block 2 upper stage design was retired in 2025 after a cascade of failures, and the "production" Block 3 vehicle hasn't flown — its first booster failed ground pressure testing in November 2025.

The strongest counterargument is SpaceX's track record of eventually delivering on ambitious goals, albeit years late. Falcon 9 ultimately achieved 165 flights in 2025 with 30+ reuses per booster. But Falcon 9 took **12 years** from first flight to 50+ flights/year, and it only reuses the first stage. Starship requires full reusability of both stages — including heat shield durability that Musk himself calls "the biggest remaining problem." Independent analysts estimate Starship will be priced at $1,000–2,500/kg for the foreseeable future, 5–12x above the thesis's sub-$200/kg requirement. The Falcon 9 precedent of prices *increasing* after reuse was demonstrated (from $61M to $70M) suggests SpaceX will capture reuse economics as margin, not pass them through.

What should concern thesis proponents most is the **compounding dependency chain**: sub-$200/kg requires ship reuse (undemonstrated) + booster reuse at high cadence (demonstrated twice, both vehicles subsequently lost) + 100+ tonne payload in reusable configuration (Block 2 achieved only 35t) + 50+ flights/year (current rate: ~5/year) + rapid turnaround (demonstrated at 4.5 months, needed at 72 hours). Each of these is individually uncertain. Multiplied together, the probability of achieving all of them within 36 months is vanishingly small. The Space Shuttle's historical trajectory — from 60 flights/year at $5.5M promised to 4.5 flights/year at $1.6B delivered — remains the most sobering comparator. Starship will likely achieve transformative launch costs eventually, but "eventually" means 5–10+ years, not 3.
