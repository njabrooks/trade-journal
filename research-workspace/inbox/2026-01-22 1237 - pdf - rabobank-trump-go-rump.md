**Type:** Marketing communication

## Summary

Markets are bewildered by the ongoing revolution rolling out from the US. Beyond underlining that this is economic statecraft, not policy, historical analogies may be of help.

On this basis, we argue for a close comparison between US President Trump and former Soviet President Gorbachev.

Gorbachev tried to reform the Soviet system from capital investment and military production towards consumption, but failed, while more radical reforms were a catastrophe.

Trump is trying to shift the US system from consumption and financialisation to capital investment and military production. In his second term, this is also now entailing radical changes. The risk of failure is again evident – and the liberal world order is already crumbling.

However, Trump's reverse perestroika could arguably also succeed. We map out how, showing which (geo)political-economy structures need to change if so.

#### 2026: another year of American revolution?

Over the past decade, we warned about a collapse of the liberal world order. In November 2024, we said the second Trump admin would shift from economic policy to statecraft, with startling outcomes. We also plotted out the Trump Plan.

The start to 2026 has been more explosive, from Venezuela and Iran to Greenland and Gaza. Indeed, 250 years since the American Revolution, a new one seems to be taking place.

Historical analogies to current US actions can be helpful: early US neomercantilism; its later imperialism; 1913; the late 1930s; or the 1945-1991 Cold War. All are valid.

However, **we argue a close comparison is 1985-1991: Trump is in many ways an inverse version of former Soviet President Gorbachev.**

Both superpower leaders introduced radical structural changes to shake up their political-economy. Gorbachev failed badly. Can Trump succeed - and what does it mean if he does or doesn't?

#### The Soviet System

The Soviet system was a centrally-planned economy under Communist Party rule. It saw 5-year plans set by Gosplan, focusing on capital stock accumulation and military production.

- **Gossnab** issued "allocation certificates" for producer goods to SOEs
- **Gosbank** (central bank) made loans as ordered, monetised deficits and losses from SOEs
- Ran multiple versions of the Ruble (cash/non-transferable/trade), which weren't fungible
- 3,000 'coefficient' internal exchange rates
- Capital controls locked liquidity in
- State ministries refined plans; SOEs implemented as output targets with profit/loss irrelevant
- Soft budget constraints
- All prices set by **Goskomsen**, which rarely changed
- No private sector, central bank monetary policy, markets, or banking system as today
- State monopoly on foreign trade

This system saw rapid initial GDP growth rates while making the USSR a military superpower. However, lack of market prices and structural inflexibility misallocated capital. Lack of competition, market incentives undermined productivity and quality. Stagnation started in the 1960s, with repressed inflation (shortages of consumer goods) evident.

#### Perestroika and glasnost

President Gorbachev emerged as Soviet leader in 1985 with the remit to reform the system. He pushed **glasnost** ("openness", meaning freedom of speech) and **perestroika** ("reconstruction") as part of a new "Socialism with a human face."

Changes included:
- Retained Gosplan and Gossnab but split up Gosbank
- Consolidated government ministries
- Allowed small-scale private-sector activity and joint-ventures with SOEs
- Retained soft budget constraints and allowed only limited price liberalisation
- Tried to unify different Rubles into one fungible currency
- Removed large denomination Ruble notes from circulation overnight to target 'shadow economy'
- Loosened state monopoly on trade a little
- Shifted to hard currency for COMECON
- Labour retained welfare benefits

**Result:** Private sector role remained small; new banking sector didn't know how to look for investment opportunities; price liberalisation was slow; profit incentives often lacking; soft budget constraints endemic; bureaucracy stifling; regular flip-flops on policy.

GDP per capita growth rose briefly but then stalled at a low level, while inflation began to rise.

**Perestroika proved too timid a set of changes to have a positive impact on a rigid Soviet system.**

**Glasnost** was easier: it allowed Soviet citizens to express unhappiness, with some calling for the end of Communist Party rule. In 1991, an attempted military coup tried to turn back the clock. Gorbachev was forced to resign, and the Soviet Union collapsed.

**Subsequent shock therapy was a catastrophe:**
- Inflation skyrocketed when prices were fully liberalised
- Savings and pensions wiped out overnight
- Firms collapsed with hard budget constraints
- Tax-take plunged
- Supply chains shattered
- Imports flooded in, destroying industry
- Ruble dived
- Mass privatisation saw rise of oligarchs and mafia
- Average life expectancy for Russian men collapsed from 64.9 (1987) to 57 (1995)
- GDP per capita collapsed two thirds in a decade

#### The post-Cold War/globalisation/GFC US

Today's post-Cold War/globalisation/GFC US economy is characterized by:

- Free-market system, but **no long-term planning or capital investment**
- Atrophied military-industrial complex despite being military hegemon
- Federal reserve-centric system with US Treasuries as key collateral for the world
- Open capital account as necessity for global architecture
- **De facto soft budget constraints** as foreigners have to buy US assets or system topples
- Government and Fed have to bail out financial sector and key private firms
- **Financialisation over productive investment** (only ~20% of US bank loans for commercial/industrial purposes)
- Free trade, as US must be net importer if dollar is global reserve currency
- Small state role in economy
- Asset prices strongly influenced by Fed (to prop them up as collateral)
- Free labour, but wages for many lag productivity, asset-price growth, sometimes even inflation
- Small social safety net
- Lower-than-past GDP growth rates despite innovation
- Rising affordability issues, wealth inequality, political polarisation
- Large fiscal and current account deficits
- High and rising private and public debt
- Decline in US military-industrial strength (can't fight two major wars at once)

**Trump wants to shift the US political-economy from consumption and finance back to capital investment and military production.**

#### яeverse perestroika, step by step

Trump's goal appears to be **"Capitalism with a national-security face"** involving:

1. **Fusion of national security and economic security strategy**
   - NSS states economic security is national security
   - Strategic direction: ensure America remains strongest, richest, most powerful country

2. **Treasury-centric system with subservient Fed**
   - Fiscal dominance and realpolitik struggles
   - Central banks' effectiveness questioned
   - Requires Treasury control of Fed or ideological alignment
   - Beyond interest rates: one-size-fits-all rate-setting, inflation targeting, QE, YCC, capital controls, swaplines, stablecoins

3. **Soft budget constraints for key sectors**
   - Foreign structural demand for US financial assets built into architecture
   - But Triffin Dilemma needs to change
   - Reduce government spending via DOGE
   - High tariff revenues
   - Target high nominal GDP growth
   - **Massive new fiscal expenditure needed for military** ($500bn Pentagon increase for 2027, $175bn+ for Golden Dome)
   - Public sector subsidies needed for economic statecraft
   - Help sustain fiscal deficits financing shift to productive investment

4. **Neomercantilism**
   - Tariffs, non-tariff barriers, export controls
   - "Balanced trade"
   - Securing critical supply chains and materials
   - Reindustrialisation
   - Reviving defense industrial base
   - Energy dominance (oil, gas, coal, nuclear)
   - Preserving/growing financial sector dominance
   - Control over global choke points and critical supply chains
   - Raw materials flow to US cheaply, priced in dollars
   - Commodities processed inside US-led bloc
   - Global sorting of who is 'in' and 'out' of bloc

5. **The 'Warsaw Pact'**
   - Replace open, values-based alliance with closed group of ideologically/economically-linked economies
   - "Burden-sharing and burden-shifting" from allies
   - 5% defence spending by NATO by 2035
   - Foster political-economy changes in allies
   - Trump wants Germany/others to raise walls on trade, capital, migration, green policies
   - NSS accuses Europe of "civilisational erasure"
   - Backs "cultivating resistance to Europe's current trajectory within European nations"
   - On glasnost: oppose "elite-driven, anti-democratic restrictions on core liberties" in allies
   - Threats over Greenland show quasi-Soviet 'intervention' under national security umbrella

6. **New economic development model**
   - Broader US economic development model to others globally
   - "Partner nations to build up domestic economies"
   - "Economically stronger Western Hemisphere becomes attractive market for US commerce/investment"
   - Cheap commodities, sellers move up industrial value chain
   - More trade at margin if ideologically aligned
   - Choice: "American-led world of sovereign countries and free economies or parallel one influenced by countries on other side of world"
   - Increase US FDI: infrastructure, critical minerals, communications networks
   - Export-credit financing
   - US rival to China's Belt and Road Initiative

7. **Capital controls: the Reverse Marshall Plan**
   - Capital restricted from flowing where not useful to national strategy
   - Compelled to flow in useful directions
   - **Trade deals struck for US market access: $7.7trn pledged FDI from trade partners**
   - Additional corporate pledges of $4.8trn
   - Past trade surpluses ploughed back into productive economy as price for security umbrella
   - US will direct capital to specific productive areas markets overlooked
   - Creates matching balance sheet trade/current account deficit US wants to avoid

8. **Dollar stablecoins**
   - Geopolitical weapon and logical tool
   - Foreign firm wants to hold stablecoin:
     - US issuer receives country's FX
     - Sells to central bank for dollars
     - Sends dollars to US Treasury for T-Bill
     - Issues stablecoin to firm
     - **Net result: dollars flow to US, US funds fiscal deficit with T-Bills at lower rates**
   - Exporter to US paid in stablecoins:
     - Sends goods to importer
     - Importer sends dollars to stablecoin issuer
     - Issuer buys T-Bill from Treasury
     - Stablecoin sent to exporter
     - **No dollars leave US, just tokens; external holding of US debt not increased**
   - US can demand stablecoin 'reinvested' back into US as part of trade deal
   - Echoes fragmented roles of Soviet 'trade' Ruble with COMECON
   - **Potentially circumvent Triffin Dilemma**
